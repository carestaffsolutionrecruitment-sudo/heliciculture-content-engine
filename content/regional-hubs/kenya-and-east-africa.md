# Snail Farming in Kenya and East Africa

Kenya and its neighbours have the right ingredients for a snail-farming sector: native giant land snails, warm humid zones along the coast and around Lake Victoria, a growing agri-enterprise culture, and an urban food market open to new proteins. Commercial heliculture in the region is still young. That is an opportunity, but it also means fewer established suppliers, buyers and regulatory precedents than in West Africa or Europe.

This guide sets out how to build a compliant, climate-resilient snail enterprise in Kenya, with notes for Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda and the wider region.

> **Legal notice:** This guide is educational and summarises regulatory frameworks as we understand them. It is not legal advice. Requirements change and are applied differently by county and country. Confirm every permit, licence and standard with the relevant authority before you buy stock, build or sell.

---

## Section 1: Regional Species and Production Framework

### Dominant species

- ***Lissachatina fulica*** (giant African land snail, formerly *Achatina fulica*) is **native to coastal East Africa**, including Kenya and Tanzania, and is the species most Kenyan snail farms work with. Within its native range it is a legitimate production species. Outside East Africa it is one of the world's most damaging invasive species, and that shapes everything about exports (Section 2).
- **Other indigenous *Achatina* species** occur in parts of East Africa. Local distribution varies, so have stock identified by a museum, university or KWS specialist before you build a breeding line around it.
- ***Archachatina marginata*** (the West African "big black") is **not native to East Africa**. Do not import it, or any other non-native snail, without written approval from the wildlife and veterinary authorities. Introducing non-native snails risks new invasive populations and parasites.
- ***Cornu aspersum*** is not established as a commercial species in the region. Its cool-climate needs could suit high-altitude units, but it would be a non-native introduction subject to the same approvals.

**Recommendation:** build production around locally sourced, correctly identified native *Achatina*-group stock, and keep a documented, captive-bred breeding line from the start.

### Environmental and housing fit

| Zone | Typical conditions | Recommended system |
|---|---|---|
| **Coast and Lake Victoria basin** | Warm (24–32 °C), humid | Shaded outdoor pens and hutch boxes; trench pens on free-draining soil |
| **Central and western highlands** (e.g. Nairobi, Kiambu, Nakuru, Kisii) | Mild days, cool nights (often 10–14 °C), distinct dry seasons | Greenhouse or insulated indoor rack systems with night-time heat retention |
| **Arid and semi-arid lands** | Hot, dry, high evaporation | Not recommended without fully enclosed, climate-controlled units and reliable water |

**Structured greenhouse and indoor racks** are the model best suited to the highlands. A polythene greenhouse with shade netting, tiered hutch boxes on ant-proofed racks, misting lines and thermal mass (water drums or block walls) to buffer cold nights can hold *Achatina* within 22–30 °C. Insulate or heat the breeding section first, because hatchlings are the most cold-sensitive.

## Section 2: Agricultural Compliance and Legal Framework

### Wildlife and environmental authorities

- **Kenya Wildlife Service (KWS).** Snails are wild animals, and KWS has historically treated snail farming as a form of wildlife farming that needs a permit or licence under the Wildlife Conservation and Management Act, 2013. Before buying stock, contact KWS for the current requirements, record-keeping obligations and any limits on sourcing founder stock from the wild.
- **Kenya Forest Service (KFS).** Collecting snails or other materials from gazetted forests requires KFS permission. Never take founder stock or substrate from forest reserves without it.
- **National Environment Management Authority (NEMA).** Larger developments, especially those with significant water use, effluent or land clearance, may need environmental screening or an Environmental Impact Assessment licence.
- **County government.** You will need a single business permit, and possibly public health and planning consent for buildings, from your county.

### Invasive species and movement controls

- **Do not move live snails** to areas or countries where the species is not established. Many importing countries prohibit live giant African land snails outright.
- **Escape-proofing is an obligation.** Even native stock must not be released or allowed to escape into areas where it is not present.
- **Moving live stock between counties or across borders** may need veterinary movement permits. Check with the Directorate of Veterinary Services.

### Export requirements

Export markets are where East African producers most often run into trouble.

- **Live snails:** most potential importers prohibit live *Lissachatina fulica*. Assume live export is not possible unless an importer confirms otherwise in writing.
- **Processed meat:** frozen or processed snail meat may be admissible in some markets, but typically only from **approved establishments** in **authorised countries**, with an official veterinary health certificate. The EU, for example, lists the countries and establishments it accepts snails from, so check the current EU list before approaching European buyers.
- **Documents:** veterinary health certificate from the Directorate of Veterinary Services, certificate of origin, commercial invoice and packing list, plus any import permit the destination requires.

### Processing standards

- **Food safety:** processing premises must meet county public health requirements and national food safety standards. Products for formal retail may need to meet Kenya Bureau of Standards (KEBS) requirements for labelling and packaging.
- **Clean processing area:** separate live-animal, raw processing and packing areas; washable surfaces; potable water; handwashing stations; cold storage at 0–5 °C (chilled) or −18 °C (frozen).
- **Process controls:** purge, chill, humane dispatch by rolling boil, removal of the visceral mass and intestine, thorough cleaning, and validated cooling and freezing (see the Snail World course, Module 5).
- **Public health:** giant African land snails can carry the rat lungworm (*Angiostrongylus cantonensis*). Always cook snails thoroughly, and wear gloves when handling them raw.
- **Mucin for cosmetics:** check the requirements for cosmetic ingredients with KEBS and with the destination market's regulator before selling.

## Section 3: Climate Adaptation and Risk Mitigation

### Micro-climate engineering

**Targets for *Achatina*-group snails:** 24–28 °C (acceptable 22–30 °C) and 80–95 % relative humidity.

- **Highland cold nights:** retain daytime heat with thermal mass and insulated night curtains, close the greenhouse vents at dusk, and place breeding boxes in the warmest zone. Consider low-wattage heating mats or a solar-charged heat source for the nursery.
- **Dry seasons (roughly January to February and June to September in much of Kenya):** increase misting, add shade netting (50–70 %), deepen moist substrate to 15–20 cm and mulch outdoor pens.
- **Coastal heat:** prioritise shade and air movement; site pens under trees or roofs; avoid metal roofing directly over boxes without insulation.
- **Heavy rains:** raise pens or improve drainage, keep run-off out of trench pens, and prevent waterlogging, which drowns hatchlings.

### Water and substrate management

- **Water supply:** use rainwater harvesting with covered tanks as the primary source where rainfall allows. Solar-powered borehole pumps provide reliable off-grid supply. Low-pressure drip lines and timed misting nozzles minimise water use. Test borehole water for salinity, because salty water harms snails.
- **Calcium sources:** agricultural lime and feed-grade limestone are widely available. Crushed oyster or marine shell should come only from licensed suppliers; never use live coral or reef material. Eggshell from local poultry farms can be sterilised and ground.
- **Substrate pH:** many highland soils are acidic. Test them and correct to about pH 7–8 with agricultural lime before use, and retest between batches.
- **Feed:** pawpaw, banana, cocoyam and kale (*sukuma wiki*) leaves, fruit waste from markets (washed, pesticide-free) and a formulated meal of maize bran, soya or sunflower cake, and limestone. **Never add salt.**

## Section 4: Local Market Access and Economic Channels

### Primary buyers

| Channel | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Hotels and high-end restaurants** (Nairobi, Mombasa, tourist circuits) | Small volumes, quality-focused; need consistent supply and food safety documentation |
| **Diaspora and expatriate communities** (including West African, Asian and European residents) | Familiar with snail dishes; live or cleaned frozen meat |
| **Direct-to-consumer and farm-gate** | Requires education: recipes, tastings and nutrition messaging |
| **Cosmetic and mucin buyers** | Emerging; requires batch documentation and testing |
| **Export brokers** | Only for processed product from approved establishments; verify admissibility first |
| **Training and breeding-stock sales** | Common in the region, but sell only correctly identified, legally held stock to buyers in areas where the species is established |

Domestic demand in Kenya is still developing. Confirm buyers before scaling, and treat consumer education as part of your marketing budget.

### Pricing, grading and economic viability

**Grading standard (live snails):**

| Grade | Live weight (indicative) | Shell |
|---|---|---|
| Small | < 80 g | Intact, firm |
| Medium | 80–150 g | Intact, firm lip |
| Large | 150 g + | Intact, thick lip |

Reject cracked, foul-smelling or non-retracting snails.

**Unit economics:** calculate your break-even price per kg as (fixed costs + variable costs per cycle) ÷ saleable kg. Use local quotes for feed, energy and labour, and confirmed buyer prices for revenue. Highland greenhouse units carry higher capital and energy costs than coastal pens, so model both before choosing a site. See Module 7 of the Snail World course for the full financial model.

**Cold chain:** cooked or cleaned meat should be chilled to 0–5 °C or frozen to −18 °C. Use insulated boxes and ice packs for local delivery, and refrigerated transport for Nairobi–Mombasa routes. Live *Achatina* should travel humid and ventilated at moderate temperatures, never chilled.

**Health equity:** snail meat is protein-rich and low in fat, and it can be produced on very small plots. Cooperative breeding and shared processing let women- and youth-led smallholder groups take part in the higher-value parts of the chain.
