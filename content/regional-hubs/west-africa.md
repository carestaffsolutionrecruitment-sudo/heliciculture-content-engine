# Snail Farming in West Africa

West Africa is the heartland of giant land snail consumption. From peppered snail at Lagos parties to snail kebabs on Ghanaian roadsides, demand is deep-rooted, and it has long been met by wild collection from forests and farm edges. Deforestation and over-harvesting are shrinking wild populations, while urban demand keeps rising. Farming fills that gap: it supplies a valued protein, creates rural enterprise and takes pressure off wild stocks.

This guide focuses on Nigeria and Ghana, the region's largest commercial hubs, with principles that apply across the forest belt from Côte d'Ivoire to Cameroon.

> **Legal notice:** This guide is educational and summarises regulatory frameworks as we understand them. It is not legal advice. Requirements change and vary by state, region and agency. Confirm every registration, permit and standard with the relevant authority.

## Section 1: Regional Species and Production Framework

### Dominant species

| Species | Local names | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| ***Archachatina marginata*** (big black snail) | *Igbin* (Yoruba), *ejula* (Igbo) | Large, meaty, highly prized; tolerates handling well | Few eggs per clutch (roughly 4–18), so stock builds slowly |
| ***Achatina achatina*** (giant tiger snail) | *Nwa* (Twi, Ghana) | Very large; lays hundreds of eggs per clutch | More sensitive to dryness and cold; slower to reach maturity |
| ***Limicolaria*** species | — | Small, prolific | Lower market value; mainly local consumption |

> **Biosecurity warning:** *Lissachatina fulica*, the East African giant snail, has been reported in parts of West Africa. It competes with native species and is a pest of crops. Learn to identify it and never farm or spread it.

**Recommendation:** combine *A. marginata* for its premium size and market preference with *A. achatina* for volume, and keep each species in separate, labelled units.

### Environmental and housing fit

| System | Description | Best use |
|---|---|---|
| **Trench pens** | Shallow trenches (about 0.5 m deep) lined with block or sheet, filled with loam, covered with mesh and shaded | Low-cost growing in humid zones |
| **Hutch boxes** | Raised wooden or block boxes with mesh lids and 15–20 cm of substrate | Breeding and nurseries; easy inspection |
| **Tyre pens** | Stacked used tyres with substrate and mesh tops | Starter and backyard units |
| **Mini-paddocks and forest-edge pens** | Fenced plots in shaded, humid microhabitats with planted forage (pawpaw, cocoyam) | Semi-intensive growing in the forest zone |
| **Indoor and greenhouse units** | Roofed, shaded sheds with misting | Commercial scale; dry-season and Harmattan resilience |

The humid forest zone, across southern Nigeria and Ghana, suits open-air trench, pen and forest-edge systems for most of the year. Further north, in the savannah, and anywhere dry seasons are long, roofed or indoor units are needed.

## Section 2: Agricultural Compliance and Legal Framework

### Business and production

- **Nigeria:** register the business with the **Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC)**, then obtain state and local government permits. The Federal Ministry of Agriculture and state agricultural development programmes offer extension support.
- **Ghana:** register with the **Office of the Registrar of Companies**, then obtain district assembly business permits. Contact the Ministry of Food and Agriculture extension services.
- **Wild collection:** wildlife laws, including closed seasons and protected areas, may restrict collecting snails from the wild. In Ghana, check with the **Forestry Commission's Wildlife Division**. Source founder stock from established farms rather than forests wherever possible.

### Import, export and movement

- **Export health certification:** exports of snail products need veterinary certification. In Nigeria this comes from the Federal Department of Veterinary and Pest Control Services, with inspection by the **Nigeria Agricultural Quarantine Service (NAQS)**. In Ghana it comes from the **Veterinary Services Directorate**.
- **Destination rules apply.** Many countries, including the United States, prohibit live giant African land snails. The EU accepts snails for human consumption only from listed countries and approved establishments. Confirm admissibility before taking an export order.
- **Diaspora shipments:** carrying or posting snails abroad without the proper certificates risks seizure, fines and reputational damage for the sector.

### Processing standards

- **Packaged and processed foods:** Nigeria requires **NAFDAC** registration for packaged food products; Ghana requires **Food and Drugs Authority (FDA)** registration. Requirements cover premises, hygiene, labelling and product testing.
- **Clean processing:** dedicated processing rooms with washable surfaces, potable water, handwashing points and separate raw and finished areas. Dispatch humanely by rolling boil before cracking the shell, remove the viscera, clean with alum or lime, and chill or freeze promptly.
- **Hygiene:** wear gloves for raw handling, always cook snails thoroughly, and use proper cold storage (0–5 °C chilled, −18 °C frozen).
- **Labelling:** product name, species, weight, batch number, dates, storage instructions, producer details and registration number.

## Section 3: Climate Adaptation and Risk Mitigation

### Micro-climate engineering

**Targets:** 24–28 °C (acceptable 22–30 °C) and 80–95 % relative humidity.

**The Harmattan** (roughly November or December to February or March) brings dry, dusty north-easterly winds, low humidity and cool nights. It is the main climate risk for West African snail farms. Without protection, snails aestivate, stop growing and die from dehydration.

- **Windbreaks:** hedges, mats or walls on the north-east side of pens.
- **Shade and cover:** shade nets and thatch over pens; cover trench and box tops with damp sacking at night.
- **Moisture:** increase watering or misting; deepen and mulch the substrate; give the snails moist burrowing material.
- **Dust:** cover feed and water points, and clean residues more often.
- **Breeding schedule:** plan main breeding and hatching for the rainy season, and hold breeders in the most protected units during the Harmattan.

**Rainy season risks:** waterlogging, drowning of hatchlings and bacterial outbreaks. Ensure drainage, raise hutches and remove residues daily.

### Water and substrate management

- **Water:** boreholes with solar pumps are increasingly affordable and avoid unreliable grid power. Combine them with rainwater harvesting and simple gravity-fed drip or misting lines. Use only clean water, and never water containing salt or chemical residues.
- **Calcium sources:** ground limestone, oyster shell from coastal suppliers, and sterilised crushed snail shell or eggshell from your own operation. Provide calcium in the feed and as free-choice grit.
- **Substrate:** loamy topsoil from a pesticide-free source, mixed with decomposed leaf litter. Test the pH and correct acidic soils (common in the forest zone) to about pH 7 with lime. Replace or sun-sterilise the substrate between batches.
- **Feed:** pawpaw leaves and fruit, cocoyam and banana leaves, cassava peels (dried and well processed; never raw leaves), and a formulated meal of maize, soya or groundnut cake, wheat offal and limestone. **No salt.**

## Section 4: Local Market Access and Economic Channels

### Primary buyers

| Channel | What they buy | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Open markets and traders** (e.g. Lagos, Ibadan, Kumasi, Accra) | Live snails by size | Highest volume; price-sensitive; seasonal |
| **Restaurants, bars and event caterers** | Live or cleaned snails | Peppered snail and party "small chops"; steady demand |
| **Hotels and high-end restaurants** | Cleaned, graded, frozen meat | Need consistency and food safety documentation |
| **Supermarkets and processors** | Registered, packaged frozen product | NAFDAC or FDA registration required |
| **Diaspora export** (UK, EU, US) | Frozen or processed meat | Only through certified, admissible routes |
| **Cosmetic laboratories** | Mucin | Emerging; batch documentation and testing needed |

### Pricing, grading and economic viability

**Grading standard (live snails):**

| Grade | Indicative live weight | Shell |
|---|---|---|
| Small | Under ~80 g | Intact, firm |
| Medium | ~80–150 g | Intact, firm lip |
| Jumbo | Over ~150 g | Intact, thick lip |

**Seasonality:** wild supply peaks in the rainy season and drops in the dry season, when prices usually rise. Farms that can supply through the Harmattan dry season command a premium. Plan batches to hit that window.

**Unit economics:** break-even price per kg = (fixed costs + variable costs per cycle) ÷ saleable kg. *A. marginata*'s long cycle and low egg output mean working capital is tied up for over a year. Budget for it, or start by buying in juveniles from a reputable breeder. Use the Snail World course financial model (Module 7) with local prices.

**Cold chain:** live snails should travel in ventilated, humid sacks or crates, shaded and never sealed airtight. Processed meat should be chilled at 0–5 °C or frozen at −18 °C in insulated boxes. Solar-powered cold rooms are an increasingly viable option for off-grid farms.

**Health equity and conservation:** farmed snails provide affordable, iron- and protein-rich food. They also reduce pressure on forest ecosystems and create rural income, particularly for women, who often lead snail trading.
