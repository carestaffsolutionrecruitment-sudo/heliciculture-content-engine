# Snail Farming in the United States and North America

North America is the most tightly regulated region for snail farming. In the United States, land snails are treated primarily as **plant pests**. Moving them requires federal permits, and the giant African land snail is effectively banned. As a result, almost all escargot sold in the US is imported, canned or frozen.

Producers can still find opportunities in permitted, contained production, in legal import and distribution, and in value-added processing. This guide explains the framework and where the realistic openings are.

> **Legal notice:** This guide is educational and summarises federal, state and Canadian frameworks as we understand them. It is not legal advice. Snail regulation in North America is strict and enforced. Obtain written confirmation from USDA APHIS, your state department of agriculture, or the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) before acquiring, moving or selling any live snails.

---

## Section 1: Regional Species and Production Framework

### Dominant species

- ***Cornu aspersum*** (brown garden snail) is the only realistic farmed species. It is already established in parts of the US, notably California, where it is an agricultural pest. Keeping or moving live *Cornu aspersum* is still regulated (Section 2).
- **Giant African land snails** (*Lissachatina fulica*, plus other *Achatina* and *Archachatina* species): **do not acquire, keep or farm them.** USDA APHIS states that it is illegal to import or possess giant African land snails in the United States. They have been the target of repeated eradication programmes in Florida, and illegal pet and food imports have been seized.
- **Native North American snails** are not commercial food species, and some are protected.

### Environmental and housing fit

Because of the permit conditions and the climate, the realistic model is **fully contained, indoor, climate-controlled production**:

- An insulated, pest-proof building with sealed openings, double-door entry and meshed drains.
- Tiered racks of lidded boxes, with misting, heating, cooling, ventilation, data loggers and alarms.
- Recirculating air handling with filtered intake and exhaust, so no snails or eggs can escape through ventilation.
- Containment of all waste. Spent substrate, rejected eggs and mortalities are frozen or heat-treated before leaving the building.

Outdoor or open-pen systems are generally incompatible with permit containment requirements and invasive-species concerns.

## Section 2: Agricultural Compliance and Legal Framework

### Federal (United States)

- **USDA APHIS Plant Protection and Quarantine (PPQ):** live snails and slugs are regulated as plant pests under the Plant Protection Act. Importing live snails, or moving them between states, needs a **PPQ permit** (commonly the PPQ 526 permit for plant pests), which typically sets strict containment conditions. Expect facility inspection and detailed conditions on movement, security and disposal.
- **Giant African land snails:** prohibited. Do not import, possess, sell or move them, live or dead, for any purpose without explicit federal authorisation.
- **Food safety (FDA):** snails for human consumption fall under the Food and Drug Administration rather than USDA's meat inspection service. Food facilities that manufacture, process, pack or hold food generally must **register with FDA** and comply with the Food Safety Modernization Act requirements that apply (such as current good manufacturing practice and preventive controls). Imported escargot must meet FDA import requirements.
- **Allergen labelling:** under US law, molluscs are **not** one of the nine major food allergens that must be declared (crustacean shellfish are). Many buyers still expect a voluntary mollusc statement, and products exported to the UK or EU must declare molluscs.

### State level

- **State departments of agriculture** may impose their own permits, quarantines or prohibitions on snails, and they generally must agree before APHIS issues a permit for their state. California, Florida, Hawaii and other states with major agricultural or invasive-species concerns are especially strict.
- **State and local health departments** license food processing, retail and restaurant supply.
- **Zoning and building codes** apply to farm buildings, processing rooms and wastewater.

### Canada

- **Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA):** live snails are regulated under the Plant Protection Act. Imports and movement require permits, and giant African land snails are prohibited.
- **Safe Food for Canadians Regulations (SFCR):** businesses that import, export or trade food between provinces generally need an SFCR licence and a preventive control plan.

### Import and export

- **Imported escargot**, canned or frozen, is the main legal supply route. Importers must meet FDA requirements in the US, or CFIA and SFCR requirements in Canada, including labelling, facility registration and import notifications.
- **Exporting** US-farmed product needs the importing country's certificates. EU buyers accept snails only from listed countries and approved establishments.

### Processing standards

- A dedicated, cleanable processing room under state or local health licensing; potable water; handwashing; separate raw and finished zones.
- Purge, chill, humane dispatch by rolling boil, evisceration (hepatopancreas removal), thorough cleaning, and validated cooling, freezing or thermal processing.
- **Canned products:** low-acid canned food rules apply, including registration of the facility and filing of the scheduled process with FDA.

## Section 3: Climate Adaptation and Risk Mitigation

### Micro-climate engineering

**Targets for *Cornu aspersum*:** 18–22 °C, relative humidity 75–90 %. Because production is indoors, the engineering challenge is building performance:

- **Cold winters (northern states, Canada):** insulate well, use thermostatic heating with redundancy, add back-up power for heating and controls, and fit alarms for temperature and power failure.
- **Hot summers (southern and interior states):** mechanical cooling, insulated roofs and pulsed misting; avoid locating rooms under uninsulated roofs.
- **Arid climates (Southwest, interior West):** very low outdoor humidity means misting demand is high. Seal the building envelope to hold humidity, and recirculate air rather than relying on constant fresh-air exchange, while still clearing ammonia and carbon dioxide.
- **Breeding:** a controlled cold rest (6–8 weeks at 5–7 °C), then 18–20 °C under long days (16–18 hours of light).

### Water and substrate management

- **Water:** municipal water must be dechlorinated, by standing or carbon filtration, before use on snails. Well water should be tested for nitrates and salinity. Recirculating humidification reduces consumption.
- **Calcium:** feed-grade limestone (calcium carbonate), oyster shell flour and dicalcium phosphate are readily available from feed suppliers.
- **Substrate:** sterilised loam or coir mixes at about pH 7–8. Heat-treat substrate before use, and contain and treat spent substrate before disposal, as the permit requires.
- **Feed:** formulated meal (about 15–18 % crude protein, 10–13 % calcium, **no salt**) plus washed, pesticide-free greens.

## Section 4: Local Market Access and Economic Channels

### Primary buyers

| Channel | What they buy | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **French and fine-dining restaurants** | Prepared escargot, fresh or frozen meat, shells | Premium for domestic, fresh product with a provenance story |
| **Specialty and gourmet retailers** | Jarred or frozen escargot, caviar | Higher margin; strong branding needed |
| **Ethnic and specialty wholesalers** | Frozen processed snail meat | West African, Caribbean and Asian communities; **must be legally sourced**, never live giant African land snails |
| **Food service distributors** | Imported canned or frozen escargot | Volume, price-driven |
| **Direct-to-consumer online** | Frozen prepared escargot, gift sets | Cold-chain shipping and state shipping rules apply |
| **Cosmetic manufacturers** | Snail secretion filtrate | Mostly imported; a domestic contained producer could offer a traceable supply |

### Pricing, grading and economic viability

**Grading standard:** *Cornu aspersum* sorted by live weight (for example small or petit-gris ~7–12 g, large or gros-gris ~15–25 g) with a fully formed, hard lip and intact shell.

**Economics:**

- **High costs:** permit compliance, contained buildings, energy and labour put US production costs well above those of imported product. Compete on freshness, provenance and quality, and sell to premium channels.
- **Lower-risk entry routes:** importing and distributing compliant escargot, or processing legally imported raw material into value-added products, avoids the live-animal permit burden.
- **Break-even price per kg** = (fixed costs + variable costs per cycle) ÷ saleable kg. Include permit and inspection costs, containment infrastructure and back-up power in the fixed costs.

**Cold chain:** frozen product at −18 °C (0 °F) or colder, chilled at 0–5 °C (32–41 °F). Ship with insulated packaging and dry ice or gel packs, and check carrier and state rules for shipping perishable food. **Never ship live snails** without the required federal and state permits.

**Responsible growth:** strict containment protects US agriculture and ecosystems from invasive snails. Compliant producers and importers can still supply a nutritious, low-footprint protein to specialty and diaspora markets.
