# Module 2: Commercial Snail Pen Design and Micro-Climate Control

**Module objective:** Design and build housing that keeps snails inside their operating band, prevents escapes and excludes predators, at a capital cost the enterprise can carry.

**Commercial outcome:** A specified, costed pen or room design and a daily monitoring routine that keeps the environment inside its targets.

## Learning Objectives

- Compare the main housing systems and choose the right one for your species, climate and budget.
- Specify materials for escape-proofing, substrate and drainage.
- Set and maintain species-specific targets for relative humidity and temperature.
- Plan lighting, ventilation and predator exclusion.

---

## Lesson 2.1: System Typologies: Free-Range, Trench, Tyre and Intensive Indoor Racks

| System | Description | Best for | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Free-range / outdoor pens** | Fenced, planted beds with shelters, stocked in the growing season | *Cornu aspersum* in temperate summers; *Achatina* in humid tropics | Low capital cost; natural forage | Seasonal; weather-dependent; harder to secure |
| **Trench pens** | Shallow trenches lined with block or sheet, filled with substrate and covered with mesh | *Achatina* in West Africa | Cheap; stable moisture | Drainage critical; harder to inspect |
| **Tyre pens** | Stacked used tyres filled with substrate, topped with mesh | Smallholder *Achatina* units | Very low cost; modular | Small scale only; heat build-up in sun; hard to clean thoroughly |
| **Hutch boxes** | Raised wooden or block boxes with mesh lids and substrate | *Achatina* breeders and nurseries | Good control; easy inspection | Timber can harbour mites; labour per box |
| **Mixed (hybrid) system** | Indoor breeding and nursery; outdoor or polytunnel fattening | *Cornu aspersum* in Europe | Year-round hatching with low-cost fattening | Two systems to manage |
| **Intensive indoor racks** | Climate-controlled rooms with tiered boxes or trays | Both groups; mandatory for *Achatina* in temperate climates | Year-round output; highest control | Highest capital and energy cost |

**Choosing a system:** match it to the species' climate needs first (Module 1), then to your capital and labour. Many successful farms start with low-cost pens and add a controlled breeding room once production is proven.

## Lesson 2.2: Materials Specification: Escape-Proofing, Substrate Composition and Drainage

**Escape-proofing.** Design for zero escapes. For *Cornu aspersum* this protects your margin; for *Achatina* it is also a legal and environmental obligation.

- **Outdoor perimeter:** HDPE snail netting or sheeting buried 20–30 cm deep and standing 50–70 cm high, with the top 10–15 cm folded outwards and down at about 45°.
- **Anti-escape wire:** a twin-strand, low-voltage (typically 9–12 V DC) wire along the inside of the fence. Keep vegetation off it.
- **Indoor units:** latched mesh lids on every box (1–2 mm aperture for hatchlings, 3–5 mm for growers), sealed wall and floor joints, meshed drains with a secondary trap, screened vents and a double-door entry.
- **Clear strip:** at least 50 cm of mown grass or gravel outside the perimeter, so escapes and predators are visible.

**Substrate.** The substrate is where snails rest, feed and lay eggs.

- **Composition:** a loose, friable loam or loam–coir mix with moderate organic matter (roughly 5–10 %), free of pesticides, sharp stones and clay lumps.
- **pH:** close to neutral or slightly alkaline (about 7–8). Correct acidity with ground limestone.
- **Moisture:** moist enough to hold together when squeezed, but never dripping.
- **Depth:** 10–20 cm for *Achatina* and in laying pots; 5–10 cm in *Cornu* growing boxes.
- **Hygiene:** sterilise or heat-treat substrate before use and replace or re-treat it between batches.

**Drainage.** Waterlogged substrate drowns hatchlings and breeds bacteria. Slope floors to drains, use a gravel base layer under outdoor beds and trench pens, and never site pens in hollows that collect run-off.

## Lesson 2.3: Controlled Micro-Climate: Managing Relative Humidity and Temperature

The targets in this module's outline (75–95 % relative humidity, 20–28 °C) suit *Achatina*-group species. They are too warm for *Cornu aspersum*, whose optimal growth temperature is 18–22 °C. Always set targets by species:

| Target | *Cornu aspersum* | *Achatina* group |
|---|---|---|
| Daytime temperature | 18–22 °C | 24–28 °C |
| Acceptable band | 15–24 °C | 22–30 °C |
| Relative humidity (active period) | 75–90 % | 80–95 % |

**How to hold the targets**

- **Humidity:** use pulsed misting (ultrasonic or high-pressure) controlled by a humidistat, or hand-misting morning and evening in low-tech pens. Wet surfaces without leaving standing water.
- **Temperature:** in the tropics, use shade netting, thatch, evaporative cooling and pen siting under trees. In temperate climates, use insulated rooms with thermostatic heating, plus summer cooling where needed.
- **Air movement:** gentle, continuous air flow at stock level stops humidity becoming stagnant, which is the main driver of bacterial disease. Recirculate room air through a fan and humidifier, and exchange enough fresh air to clear ammonia and carbon dioxide.
- **Monitoring:** use calibrated data loggers at stock level, not just at the controller. Indoor units should have alarms for out-of-range readings and power failure, and *Achatina* rooms need back-up power.

## Lesson 2.4: Lighting, Ventilation and Predator Exclusion Systems

**Lighting.** Snails are mostly active in darkness and at dusk, but photoperiod regulates reproduction. For *Cornu aspersum* breeders, a controlled rest of 6–8 weeks at about 5–7 °C, followed by 18–20 °C under long days (about 16–18 hours of light), reliably triggers mating and laying. Growing units can run on natural or 12–14 hour lighting. Avoid direct sun on boxes and pens.

**Ventilation.** Size mechanical ventilation to remove ammonia and excess carbon dioxide without drying the stock. Screen every air inlet with ≤1 mm insect mesh. Watch for condensation dripping from ceilings, which waterlogs boxes below.

**Predator exclusion.**

| Predator | Exclusion measure |
|---|---|
| Rats and mice | Rodent-proof building envelope, bait stations outside the perimeter, no spilt feed |
| Birds | Mesh netting over outdoor pens |
| Ants (attack eggs and hatchlings) | Water or oil moats around rack legs; sticky barriers |
| Ground beetles, centipedes | Clean surroundings; sealed indoor units |
| Snakes, lizards, shrews (tropical) | Fine mesh pen walls and lids; cleared surroundings |
| Agrochemicals from neighbours | Buffer zones; written agreements; no molluscicides on site |

## SOP / Technical Focus: Daily Micro-Climate Logging and Substrate Testing

**Daily (morning and evening):**

1. Read the temperature and relative humidity at stock level in each unit, and record both with the time and your initials.
2. Compare each reading with the species targets. If it's out of range, correct it (misting, shading, ventilation or heating), recheck after one hour and record the action.
3. Inspect boxes or pens for condensation, standing water, dry substrate and the proportion of snails sealed with an epiphragm.
4. Check the anti-escape wire, lids and door seals.

**Weekly:**

5. Test substrate moisture with the squeeze test in three places per unit.
6. Download the data-logger records and review the minimum and maximum readings against the targets.

**Monthly, and between batches:**

7. Test substrate pH with a calibrated meter or test kit, three samples per unit. Correct anything below pH 7 with ground limestone, mix it in, and retest after a week.
8. Calibrate hygrometers and thermometers against a reference instrument, and record the result.

**Escalation:** if a reading stays out of range for more than 24 hours, or more than 10 % of a growing unit is sealed, notify the farm manager and record the cause and the corrective action.
