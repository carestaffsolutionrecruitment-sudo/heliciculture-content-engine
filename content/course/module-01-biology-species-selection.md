# Module 1: Snail Biology, Species Selection and Adaptation

**Module objective:** Understand how edible land snails live, grow and respond to their environment, and choose the species that fits your climate, market and legal setting.

**Commercial outcome:** A documented species decision, justified by climate data, market demand and regulatory status, before any money is spent on infrastructure or stock.

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

- Describe the key anatomical systems of a land snail and explain how each affects husbandry.
- Explain the environmental triggers for dormancy (aestivation and hibernation) and how to prevent unwanted dormancy in production.
- Describe the growth stages of commercial species and their calcium and protein requirements.
- Score candidate species against market demand, climate suitability and legal status using the Species Comparison Matrix.

## Lesson 1.1: Gastropod Anatomy and Physiology

Land snails are terrestrial molluscs of the class Gastropoda. Their bodies are built around three features that drive every husbandry decision: a muscular **foot** used for locomotion and the part we eat; a **visceral mass** coiled inside the shell and containing the digestive gland (hepatopancreas), reproductive organs and heart; and the **mantle**, a sheet of tissue that secretes the shell and forms the lung (pulmonary cavity).

Snails breathe air through a single opening, the **pneumostome**, which they open and close to balance gas exchange against water loss. Their skin is permeable and covered in mucus, so they lose water rapidly in dry air. This makes humidity the single most important variable in any snail unit. Mucus also lubricates movement, carries chemical signals between snails and protects the body from abrasion and pathogens.

Commercial land snails are **simultaneous hermaphrodites**: each individual carries both male and female organs. Most species still need a partner, and mating is reciprocal, so every adult in a breeding group can lay eggs. Feeding uses a **radula**, a ribbon of microscopic teeth that rasps plant tissue and softened feed meal.

| Feature | *Cornu aspersum* | *Archachatina marginata* | *Achatina achatina* | *Lissachatina fulica* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Adult shell length | 25–40 mm | 120–200 mm | Up to ~200 mm | 50–200 mm |
| Typical adult weight | 7–25 g | 100–300 g+ | 100–300 g+ | 30–100 g+ |
| Eggs per clutch | ~80–120, small | ~4–18, large | Hundreds, small | ~100–400, small |
| Native range | Mediterranean and Atlantic Europe | West and Central Africa | West Africa | East Africa (now invasive worldwide) |
| Farming status | Widely farmed | Widely farmed in West Africa | Farmed in West Africa | **Restricted or prohibited in many countries** |

> **Key point:** Learn to identify *Lissachatina fulica* even if you never farm it. Its shell is more slender and conical than *Archachatina marginata*'s, and it is a biosecurity risk in mixed or imported stock.

**Practical actions**

- Practise identifying the pneumostome, foot, mantle edge and shell lip on live specimens.
- Record the species and source of every stock purchase from day one.

## Lesson 1.2: Environmental Triggers: Aestivation, Epiphragm Formation and Moisture Thresholds

Snails are ectotherms: their body temperature and activity follow their surroundings. When conditions move outside their tolerance band, they protect themselves by becoming dormant. They withdraw into the shell and seal the aperture with an **epiphragm**, a membrane of dried mucus that is often hardened with calcium carbonate.

- **Aestivation** is dormancy triggered by heat and drought. In *Cornu aspersum* it typically begins above about 27 °C, especially when relative humidity falls. *Achatina*-group snails aestivate in tropical dry seasons and when humidity drops below roughly 75–80 %.
- **Hibernation** is dormancy triggered by cold. *Cornu aspersum* typically becomes dormant below 5–7 °C. Tropical species do not hibernate safely: prolonged exposure below about 15 °C harms them.

A dormant snail does not feed or grow. In production, unplanned dormancy means lost weeks, poorer feed conversion and higher mortality. The same response can be used deliberately: a controlled cold rest for *Cornu aspersum* breeders synchronises reproduction (Module 2), and a short chill before processing produces calm, sealed snails for humane dispatch (Module 5).

**Indicative operating thresholds**

| Parameter | *Cornu aspersum* | *Achatina* group |
|---|---|---|
| Optimal growth temperature | 18–22 °C | 24–28 °C |
| Acceptable band | 15–24 °C | 22–30 °C |
| Cold dormancy risk | Below 5–7 °C | Harm below ~15 °C (sustained) |
| Heat / aestivation risk | Above ~27 °C | Above ~32 °C |
| Relative humidity (active period) | 75–90 % | 80–95 % |

**Practical actions**

- Treat widespread epiphragm formation in a growing unit as an alarm, not a curiosity. Check humidity, temperature and water supply the same day.
- Never pull an epiphragm off a dormant snail. Restore conditions and let it emerge naturally.

## Lesson 1.3: Growth Stages, Shell Calcification and Nutritional Requirements

Commercial snails pass through four production stages:

1. **Egg and incubation.** Eggs are laid in moist soil. *Cornu aspersum* eggs hatch in roughly 2–4 weeks depending on temperature; *Achatina*-group eggs in roughly 3–6 weeks.
2. **Hatchling (nursery).** Hatchlings eat their own eggshells first, an important early calcium source. They are fragile, drown easily and need fine mesh and high hygiene.
3. **Grower (fattening).** The phase of fastest weight gain and greatest feed use. Most of the production cycle is spent here.
4. **Adult and breeder.** In bordered species such as *Cornu aspersum*, maturity is marked by a thickened, flared **shell lip** (peristome). Growth in shell length stops, and reproduction begins.

The shell is mostly **calcium carbonate** laid down by the mantle edge on an organic matrix. Without sufficient dietary calcium, shells become thin and fragile, growth slows and egg production falls. Calcium must be available continuously, not occasionally.

**Indicative nutritional targets (dry-matter basis)**

| Nutrient | *Cornu aspersum* growers | *Achatina* growers |
|---|---|---|
| Crude protein | 15–18 % | 18–22 % |
| Calcium | 10–13 % | 8–12 % |
| Added salt | **None** (salt is toxic to snails) | **None** |

**Time to market (controlled conditions):** *Cornu aspersum* roughly 4–7 months from hatching; *Achatina*-group species roughly 12–24 months.

**Practical actions**

- Weigh a sample of 30–50 snails per batch every two weeks and record the average weight. This growth curve is your most important management signal.

## Lesson 1.4: Species Selection Matrix: Market Demand vs Climate Suitability

Choose the species before you design anything else. The right species depends on four factors, in this order:

1. **Legal status.** Is it legal to keep, farm and sell this species where you are? If not, stop there.
2. **Climate suitability.** Can you hold the species within its operating band all year, and at what energy cost?
3. **Market demand.** Who will buy it, in what form and at what price? Confirm buyers before stocking.
4. **Production economics.** Cycle length, reproduction rate, feed cost and mortality risk.

As a rule of thumb:

- **UK, Europe and temperate climates:** *Cornu aspersum*, which is legal, adapted to the climate and has an established market. *Achatina* only in fully enclosed, heated buildings, with legal advice and a confirmed buyer.
- **West and Central Africa:** native *Archachatina marginata* and *Achatina achatina*, which have strong local demand and suit the climate with simple shaded pens.
- **Everywhere:** never introduce a non-native species into an area where it could become invasive.

## SOP / Technical Focus: Stock Health Assessment on Arrival

1. **Verify documents.** Confirm the species, supplier, origin (captive-bred or wild), quantity and any health or movement certificates. Reject undocumented stock.
2. **Move to quarantine.** Place the consignment straight into the quarantine room (Module 4), never the main unit.
3. **Inspect a sample.** Check at least 10 % of the snails, or all of them if there are fewer than 50, for shell damage, abnormal mucus, foul smell, retraction failure and visible mites.
4. **Count the dead.** Count dead and moribund snails, and record the percentage.
5. **Weigh a sample.** Weigh 30 snails individually and record the mean and range.
6. **Decide.** Accept, accept with a treatment plan, or reject and return. Record the decision and the reason.
