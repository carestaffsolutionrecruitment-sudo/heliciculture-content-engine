# Intensive Heliculture: Pen Design, Temperature Thresholds, and Biosecurity Standards

Intensive snail farming rewards precision and punishes improvisation. A well-run unit converts low-cost plant protein and calcium into a premium meat product with a short supply chain; a poorly run one loses stock to dehydration, disease, predators and escape — and, in the case of tropical species, can create a genuine invasive-species liability.

This guide sets out the operational standards we recommend for commercial producers in the UK and continental Europe. It compares the two species groups most often proposed for intensive systems — the European brown garden snail (*Cornu aspersum*) and the giant African land snails (*Achatina*, *Archachatina* and *Lissachatina* species) — and covers pen and room design, environmental thresholds, escape prevention, feeding formulae and a working biosecurity programme.

> **Key takeaways**
>
> - *Cornu aspersum* is the default commercial species for UK and European producers. It tolerates temperate conditions, has an established market (*petit-gris* and *gros-gris*) and carries a far lower regulatory and biosecurity burden than *Achatina*.
> - *Achatina* species need a stable 22–30 °C and high humidity. In the UK and most of Europe they can only be produced in fully enclosed, heated buildings, and the escape-prevention standard must be absolute.
> - Escape prevention is a design discipline, not an afterthought: buried and folded perimeter barriers, low-voltage anti-escape wires, mesh lids, sealed drainage and a double-door entry protocol.
> - Commercial feed should provide roughly 15–20 % crude protein and 10–13 % calcium on a dry-matter basis, with no added salt.
> - Biosecurity rests on zoning, quarantine of incoming stock, daily removal of mortalities and rigorous record-keeping.

## 1. Species Selection: *Cornu aspersum* vs *Achatina*

Species choice determines every downstream decision: building specification, energy budget, feed formulation, market positioning and legal exposure. Decide it first and decide it on evidence.

| Attribute | *Cornu aspersum* (petit-gris / gros-gris) | *Achatina* group (*A. achatina*, *Archachatina marginata*, *Lissachatina fulica*) |
|---|---|---|
| Native range | Mediterranean and Atlantic Europe; long naturalised in Britain | Sub-Saharan Africa (*L. fulica*: East Africa, now invasive across the tropics) |
| Typical market weight | Petit-gris 7–12 g; gros-gris (*C. a. maxima*) 15–25 g | 100–300 g+ depending on species and age |
| Time to market (controlled conditions) | Roughly 4–7 months from hatching | Roughly 12–24 months from hatching |
| Clutch characteristics | Around 80–120 small eggs per laying | *A. achatina*: hundreds of eggs per clutch; *A. marginata*: few, large eggs |
| Primary markets | French, Italian, Spanish and Portuguese food trade; UK fine dining | West African diaspora retail and catering; some European ethnic markets |
| Biosecurity risk outside native range | Low to moderate (already present in the UK) | High — *L. fulica* is listed among the world's most damaging invasive species |
| Zoonotic concern | Low when farmed on controlled feed | Recognised host of the rat lungworm *Angiostrongylus cantonensis* in parts of its range |

**Our recommendation:** for UK and European producers, build the business around *Cornu aspersum*. Consider *Achatina* only where there is a confirmed buyer, a fully enclosed heated building, legal advice on the species concerned and an operator prepared to run a closed-system biosecurity regime. Never farm *Lissachatina fulica* outside a closed, licensed or otherwise authorised setting, and check the current position with your national authority before acquiring any *Achatina* stock — several jurisdictions, including the United States and Australia, prohibit them outright.

## 2. Regulatory Context for UK and European Producers

Regulation is jurisdiction-specific and changes over time. Treat the points below as a checklist of the frameworks you must review, not as legal advice.

- **Release of non-native species (Great Britain).** Section 14 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 makes it an offence to release, or allow to escape into the wild, any animal not ordinarily resident in Great Britain. For *Achatina* this means every escape is potentially an offence, not merely a commercial loss. Equivalent provisions apply in Northern Ireland and across EU member states.
- **Protected species.** The Roman or Burgundy snail (*Helix pomatia*) is protected in Great Britain under Schedule 5 of the 1981 Act and listed in Annex V of the EU Habitats Directive. Wild collection of *Helix pomatia* breeding stock is therefore restricted or prohibited in many jurisdictions; obtain it only from legitimate farmed sources where its production is permitted.
- **Food hygiene.** In Great Britain and the EU, snails intended for human consumption fall under Regulation (EC) No 853/2004 (retained as assimilated law in GB), Annex III, Section XI. Snails must be killed in an establishment, and the hepatopancreas must be removed where it may present a hazard. You will also need food business registration with your local authority (in England, at least 28 days before trading) and a documented HACCP-based food safety management system.
- **Animal by-products.** Mortalities, rejected stock, shells and processing waste are animal by-products under Regulation (EC) No 1069/2009 and its GB equivalent. Agree a compliant disposal route before stocking.
- **Animal welfare.** Gastropods currently fall outside the scope of the Animal Welfare Act 2006 and the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022. That is a gap in the law, not a licence: buyers, retailers and certification bodies increasingly expect documented humane handling and dispatch. See our companion guide, *The Science of Purging and Enzymatic Cleaning*.
- **Planning and environmental permits.** Polytunnels, insulated buildings and drainage works may need planning consent. Wash-water discharge can require an environmental permit or a trade-effluent agreement.

## 3. Production System Design

### 3.1 The three system types

1. **Fully indoor intensive (closed cycle).** Breeding, hatching, nursery and fattening all take place in climate-controlled rooms using tiered racks, boxes or trays. Highest capital and energy cost, highest control, year-round output. Mandatory for *Achatina* in temperate climates.
2. **Mixed system (the "Italian/French hybrid").** Breeding and nursery stages are indoors under controlled light and temperature; juveniles are transferred to outdoor or polytunnel fattening pens with growing vegetation for the summer season. The most common model for *Cornu aspersum* in Europe.
3. **Extensive outdoor.** Open-field pens with minimal climate control. Low cost, but seasonal, weather-dependent and difficult to secure. Not suitable for *Achatina* under any circumstances.

### 3.2 Indoor room specification (both species groups)

- **Building envelope:** insulated, rodent-proof structure with sealed wall–floor junctions, smooth washable internal surfaces and no gaps above 2 mm around services, doors or vents.
- **Floor and drainage:** sloped concrete or resin floor to a central drain. Fit drains with fine stainless mesh (≤2 mm) *and* a secondary trap so that neither snails nor eggs can enter the drainage system.
- **Racking:** stainless steel or powder-coated racks holding polypropylene or HDPE boxes with close-fitting mesh lids (1–2 mm aperture for hatchlings, 3–5 mm for growers). Avoid untreated timber, which harbours mites and is hard to disinfect.
- **Climate control:** thermostatically controlled heating (and cooling for summer peaks), ultrasonic or high-pressure misting linked to a humidistat, and mechanical ventilation sized to remove ammonia and excess CO₂ without drying the stock. Insect screening (≤1 mm) on every air inlet.
- **Lighting:** programmable LED lighting so that photoperiod can be used as a production tool (see Section 4.3).
- **Monitoring:** calibrated data loggers for temperature and relative humidity at stock level, not just at the controller, with remote alarms for out-of-range readings and power failure. Back-up power is strongly advised for *Achatina* units.

### 3.3 Outdoor and polytunnel fattening pens (*Cornu aspersum* only)

- **Bed layout:** planted beds 1–1.5 m wide separated by maintenance paths, so every part of the bed can be reached without stepping on it.
- **Vegetation:** a mixed cover crop (for example rape, radish, clover and chard) provides shade, humidity and supplementary forage. Sow four to six weeks before stocking.
- **Shelter:** inverted-V boards or shade netting raised 10–20 cm above the soil gives daytime refuge and concentrates snails for inspection.
- **Irrigation:** low-volume overhead or mist irrigation operated in the evening and early morning, when snails are active and evaporation is lowest.
- **Soil:** free-draining, calcium-rich loam with a pH around 7. Avoid waterlogged ground, which drives up bacterial disease and drowning losses.

### 3.4 *Achatina* pens and enclosures

*Achatina* are heavier, stronger and longer-lived than *Cornu* and require a different enclosure design:

- Solid-sided pens or boxes (concrete block, HDPE or stainless) with lids of welded mesh secured by latches, not loose weights. A large adult can push a light lid aside.
- 10–20 cm of moist, sterilised loam or coir substrate for burrowing and egg-laying, replaced or heat-treated between batches.
- Separate egg-incubation boxes so that eggs can be counted, dated and controlled.
- Ant exclusion around rack legs (water or oil moats, or sticky barriers), as ants prey on eggs and hatchlings.

## 4. Environmental Controls and Temperature Thresholds

Snails are ectotherms that regulate water loss behaviourally. When conditions move outside their tolerance band they seal the shell aperture with a mucous epiphragm and become dormant — hibernation in cold conditions, aestivation in hot or dry ones. A dormant snail does not eat or grow. Environmental control is therefore a direct driver of feed conversion and time to market.

### 4.1 Indicative operating thresholds

The ranges below are drawn from published husbandry literature and producer practice. They are indicative: calibrate them to your strain, stage and system using your own production records.

| Parameter | *Cornu aspersum* | *Achatina* group |
|---|---|---|
| Optimal air temperature (growth) | 18–22 °C | 24–28 °C |
| Acceptable operating band | 15–24 °C | 22–30 °C |
| Activity slows / hibernation risk | Below ~10 °C; dormancy typically below 5–7 °C | Below ~18 °C; prolonged exposure below ~15 °C is harmful |
| Aestivation / heat stress risk | Above ~27 °C, especially with low humidity | Above ~32 °C, especially with low humidity |
| Relative humidity (active period) | 75–90 % | 80–95 % |
| Substrate moisture | Moist, never waterlogged | Moist, never waterlogged |
| Photoperiod for growth and reproduction | Long day (around 16–18 h light) after a cold rest period | 12–14 h light; tolerant of tropical day length |

### 4.2 Managing humidity without disease

High humidity and high bacterial load go hand in hand. The goal is a moist micro-environment with moving air, not stagnant saturation.

- Mist in short pulses rather than continuously, keeping surfaces damp but free of standing water.
- Maintain gentle, continuous air movement at stock level.
- Remove uneaten feed and droppings daily; fermenting residue is the main driver of bacterial outbreaks.
- Watch for condensation dripping from ceilings onto boxes, which causes localised waterlogging.

### 4.3 Photoperiod and the breeding cycle (*Cornu aspersum*)

Breeding stock of *Cornu aspersum* benefit from an artificial winter. A controlled rest period of roughly six to eight weeks at around 5–7 °C, followed by a return to 18–20 °C under long-day lighting (around 16–18 hours), reliably synchronises mating and egg-laying. This allows the producer to schedule hatching batches across the year rather than relying on the natural spring cycle.

## 5. Escape Prevention

Escape is the single most important biosecurity control in heliculture. For *Cornu aspersum* it is primarily a commercial loss; for *Achatina* it is an environmental and legal risk. Design for zero escapes, then verify.

### 5.1 Outdoor and polytunnel perimeters (*Cornu aspersum*)

- **Buried barrier:** HDPE snail netting or sheeting buried 20–30 cm into the soil to prevent burrowing under the fence.
- **Above-ground height:** 50–70 cm, with the top 10–15 cm folded outwards and downwards at roughly 45° to create an overhang that snails cannot climb past.
- **Anti-escape wire:** a twin-strand, low-voltage (typically 9–12 V DC) anti-escape wire run along the inside of the fence. Snails contacting both strands receive a mild deterrent stimulus and turn back. Check continuity daily; vegetation touching the wire shorts it out.
- **Clear strip:** a mown or gravelled strip of at least 50 cm outside the fence, which makes escapes visible and deters predators.
- **Predator exclusion:** fine-mesh netting over the top of pens where bird predation is significant, and rodent control around the perimeter.

### 5.2 Indoor and *Achatina* units

- **Primary containment:** every box or pen has a latched, mesh-covered lid with no gap exceeding the smallest hatchling's shell diameter.
- **Secondary containment:** the room itself is snail-tight — sealed penetrations, door sweeps, meshed drains and screened vents.
- **Tertiary containment:** a lobby or double-door entry, so that both doors are never open at the same time. A copper or salt-free mechanical barrier strip at the threshold is a useful backstop.
- **Waste and substrate:** all spent substrate, rejected eggs and mortalities are frozen or heat-treated before leaving the building, so that viable eggs or hatchlings cannot leave in the waste stream.
- **Stock reconciliation:** count stock in and out of each room. For *Achatina*, maintain a written inventory so that unexplained losses trigger a search.
- **Documented escape-response procedure:** who searches, where, how far beyond the perimeter, and when the relevant authority is notified.

## 6. Commercial Feeding Formulae

Snails need energy, protein and — above all — calcium for shell growth. A formulated dry meal fed in troughs, supplemented with fresh vegetation, outperforms vegetation alone in growth rate, shell quality and uniformity.

### 6.1 Nutritional targets (dry-matter basis)

| Nutrient | *Cornu aspersum* (growers) | *Achatina* (growers) |
|---|---|---|
| Crude protein | 15–18 % | 18–22 % |
| Calcium | 10–13 % | 8–12 % |
| Phosphorus | 0.5–0.8 % | 0.5–0.8 % |
| Crude fibre | Below ~8 % | Below ~10 % |
| Added salt (NaCl) | **None** | **None** |

Salt is toxic to snails and must never be added to feed, supplements or cleaning regimes for live stock.

### 6.2 Example grower formula — *Cornu aspersum* (per 100 kg)

| Ingredient | kg |
|---|---|
| Ground maize | 28 |
| Soya bean meal (approx. 46 % CP) | 25 |
| Wheat bran | 12 |
| Dried lucerne meal | 4 |
| Calcium carbonate (feed-grade limestone) | 28 |
| Dicalcium phosphate | 2 |
| Vitamin–mineral premix (salt-free) | 1 |
| **Total** | **100** |

*Approximate analysis:* around 16–17 % crude protein and 11–12 % calcium.

### 6.3 Example grower formula — *Achatina* (per 100 kg)

| Ingredient | kg |
|---|---|
| Ground maize | 31 |
| Soya bean meal (approx. 46 % CP) | 30 |
| Wheat offal / middlings | 13 |
| Calcium carbonate or ground oyster shell | 20 |
| Bone meal (sterilised, compliant source) | 5 |
| Vitamin–mineral premix (salt-free) | 1 |
| **Total** | **100** |

*Approximate analysis:* around 19 % crude protein and 9 % calcium.

These are starting formulations. Adjust them on the basis of a laboratory analysis of your actual ingredients and your measured growth rates, and buy only feed-grade ingredients from suppliers registered under feed hygiene rules. Check animal by-product rules before including any animal-derived ingredient; requirements differ between Great Britain, Northern Ireland and the EU.

### 6.4 Feeding practice

- Feed dry meal in shallow troughs or on trays, not scattered on the soil, so that consumption can be measured and residues removed.
- Feed in the late afternoon or at the start of the dark period, when snails become active.
- Remove uneaten meal and wilted vegetation every day. Discard any feed that is damp, caked or mouldy — mycotoxins are a significant hidden cause of poor performance.
- Supply fresh greens (for example lucerne, clover, chard, cabbage leaves and carrot tops for *Cornu*; banana or pawpaw leaves and fruit, where available, for *Achatina*) washed and free from pesticide residues.
- Provide water by misting and through saturated sponges or very shallow dishes. Hatchlings drown easily in open water.
- Separate stock by size; larger snails outcompete smaller ones at the trough.

## 7. Biosecurity Standards

### 7.1 Principal threats

| Threat | Examples | Key control |
|---|---|---|
| Bacterial disease | *Pseudomonas* spp. and other opportunistic bacteria, often linked to wet, dirty conditions | Hygiene, ventilation, removal of residues, lower stocking density |
| Parasites and pests | Snail mites (*Riccardoella* spp.), parasitic flies, nematodes | Quarantine, clean substrate, screening, avoid untreated timber |
| Predators | Rats, mice, shrews, birds, ground beetles, ants (eggs and hatchlings) | Perimeter design, rodent control, ant barriers |
| Agrochemicals | Metaldehyde or ferric phosphate slug pellets, nematode biocontrols from neighbouring land | Buffer zones, written agreements with neighbours, no molluscicides on site |
| Zoonoses (*Achatina*) | *Angiostrongylus cantonensis* where stock originates from endemic areas | Use only known, captive-bred stock; gloves and hand-washing; no raw consumption |

### 7.2 Core biosecurity programme

1. **Zoning.** Divide the site into clean (breeding, hatching, nursery), production (fattening) and dirty (dispatch, waste, deliveries) zones. Staff and equipment move from clean to dirty, never the reverse, without cleaning and disinfection.
2. **Quarantine.** Hold all incoming stock in a separate room for at least four to six weeks, inspecting it for mortality, mites and abnormal behaviour before it joins the main population. Source breeding stock from a single, documented farm wherever possible.
3. **Hygiene.** Wash and disinfect boxes, trays and troughs between batches using a product approved for use in food-animal environments, followed by a thorough rinse — snails are highly sensitive to chemical residues.
4. **Mortality management.** Remove dead snails daily, record numbers by unit, and dispose of them through an approved animal by-product route. A sustained rise in mortality is your earliest disease signal.
5. **People and vehicles.** Dedicated footwear and protective clothing for each zone, a visitor log and no unnecessary access to breeding rooms.
6. **Records.** Keep batch records covering origin, hatch dates, stocking densities, feed lot numbers, temperature and humidity logs, treatments, mortalities and sales. These underpin traceability, food safety audits and any insurance claim.

### 7.3 Indicative stocking densities

| Stage | *Cornu aspersum* | *Achatina* |
|---|---|---|
| Hatchlings / nursery | Up to 1,500–2,000 per m² of usable surface (boxes, trays) | Up to ~100 per m² |
| Growers / fattening | 300–500 per m² (outdoor pens); higher on vertical surfaces indoors | 20–50 per m² |
| Breeders | 100–150 per m² | 5–10 per m² |

Overstocking slows growth through competition and a pheromone-mediated crowding effect, and increases disease pressure. Reduce density first when growth stalls.

## 8. Economic Realism

Intensive heliculture is a viable enterprise but not a passive one. Plan for:

- **Capital costs** concentrated in buildings, climate control, racking and fencing.
- **Energy costs** that dominate indoor *Achatina* production in a temperate climate.
- **Labour** for daily feeding, cleaning, inspection and record-keeping — snails cannot be left unattended for long periods.
- **A learning curve** of one to two production cycles before mortality and feed conversion stabilise.
- **Route to market** secured before scaling: processors, restaurants, specialist retailers or direct sales of prepared product. Live-snail sales alone rarely sustain a commercial unit.

Start with a pilot unit sized to your confirmed demand, measure everything, and scale only once performance data justify it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I farm giant African land snails (*Achatina*) in the UK?
Some *Achatina* species are legally kept in the UK, but releasing or allowing them to escape into the wild is an offence under Section 14 of the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Commercial production is only realistic in a fully enclosed, heated building with absolute escape prevention. Take legal advice and confirm the status of the specific species with the relevant authority before acquiring stock.

### What temperature is best for farming *Cornu aspersum*?
*Cornu aspersum* grows best at around 18–22 °C with 75–90 % relative humidity. Activity slows below about 10 °C, dormancy typically sets in below 5–7 °C, and heat stress and aestivation become a risk above about 27 °C, particularly when humidity is low.

### How do I stop snails escaping from outdoor pens?
Use a perimeter barrier buried 20–30 cm into the soil and standing 50–70 cm high, with the top folded outwards at about 45°. Add a twin-strand, low-voltage anti-escape wire along the inside, keep vegetation clear of the wire, and maintain a mown or gravelled strip outside the fence so escapes are easy to spot.

### What should commercial snail feed contain?
A grower meal for *Cornu aspersum* should provide roughly 15–18 % crude protein and 10–13 % calcium on a dry-matter basis; *Achatina* growers need around 18–22 % protein and 8–12 % calcium. Feed must contain no added salt and should be supplemented with clean, fresh vegetation.

### Do snail farms in the UK need to register as a food business?
Yes. If you sell snails for human consumption you must register as a food business with your local authority (in England, at least 28 days before trading) and comply with food hygiene law, including the specific requirements for snails in Annex III, Section XI of Regulation (EC) No 853/2004 as retained in Great Britain.

### How long does it take to raise snails to market weight?
Under controlled conditions, *Cornu aspersum* typically reaches market weight in roughly four to seven months from hatching. *Achatina* species are much slower, generally taking 12–24 months depending on species, temperature and feed quality.
