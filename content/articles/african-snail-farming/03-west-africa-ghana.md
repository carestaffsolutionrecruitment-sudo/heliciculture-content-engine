# West Africa II: Snail Farming in Ghana and the Coastal Belt

> **Part 3 of the series:** *[Snail Farming Across African Regions](./00-series-hub.md)*

**Key takeaways**

- Ghana has one of the oldest **snail-eating cultures** in West Africa. The snail (*nwa* in Twi) is a prized bush food, and the giant tiger snail ***Achatina achatina*** is its most celebrated species.
- Ghana's **forest belt** (Ashanti, Eastern, Western, Western North, Central, Ahafo and Bono) is excellent snail country. The **Accra Plains** and the **north** are not, even though Accra is the biggest market.
- The **Dahomey Gap**, a dry savanna corridor that reaches the coast in eastern Ghana, Togo and Benin, makes coastal snail farming harder than its latitude suggests.
- **Côte d'Ivoire** is one of the region's largest snail markets and shares Ghana's preference for *A. achatina*.

---

## 1. Ghana's Ecological Zones

| Zone | Regions / areas | Annual rainfall (approx.) | Rainfall pattern | Outdoor GALS suitability |
|------|-----------------|---------------------------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **Wet evergreen and moist evergreen forest** | Western, Western North, south-west Central | 1,700–2,200 mm | Two rainy seasons a year | ★★★★★ Ideal, including for *A. achatina* |
| **Moist semi-deciduous forest** | Ashanti (Kumasi), Eastern (Koforidua), Ahafo, Bono, Central (inland), Volta (northern highlands) | 1,300–1,700 mm | Two rainy seasons; short dry spell in August | ★★★★☆ Very good. Irrigate for the main dry season. |
| **Forest–savanna transition** | Bono East (Techiman, Kintampo), northern Ashanti, Afram Plains | 1,100–1,400 mm | Moving towards a single rainy season | ★★★☆☆ Workable with *A. marginata* and shading |
| **Coastal savanna (Accra Plains)** | Greater Accra, coastal Central (Winneba), south Volta (Ada, Keta) | **700–1,000 mm** | Two short rainy seasons; long dry spells | ★★☆☆☆ **Marginal.** Needs housing or irrigation. |
| **Guinea savanna** | Northern, Savannah, North East, northern Bono East, northern Volta (Oti) | 1,000–1,200 mm | One rainy season; Harmattan | ★☆☆☆☆ Unsuitable outdoors |
| **Sudan savanna** | Upper East, Upper West | 800–1,000 mm | One short rainy season; severe Harmattan | ☆☆☆☆☆ Not viable without full indoor control |

### The Accra paradox

Greater Accra is Ghana's **largest consumer market**, yet it lies in the driest part of the south. The Accra Plains form the western end of the **Dahomey Gap**, where savanna reaches the sea because the coastline runs parallel to the prevailing monsoon winds and wrings less rain from them. A pen in Kasoa, Tema or Dodowa faces months of low humidity that a pen near Kumasi never sees.

**Practical options for Accra-based farmers**

1. **Farm in the forest belt and truck to Accra.** The Eastern Region (Aburi, Akuapem Ridge, Koforidua, Nkawkaw) is within easy reach and much wetter. The Akuapem Ridge benefits from altitude-induced mist.
2. **Farm in Accra using housed systems**: tiered hutches in an insulated, shaded room with evening misting. See [Mitigation Systems](./09-mitigation-systems.md).
3. **Run an Accra distribution and processing hub** supplied by contract growers in the Ashanti and Eastern regions.

## 2. Species Choice in Ghana

| Species | Recommendation |
|---------|----------------|
| ***Achatina achatina*** | **Ghana's signature species** and the most prized in local markets. Choose it **only in the forest belt**, or inside humid housing. Expect low hatchling survival unless the nursery is managed carefully. |
| ***Archachatina marginata*** | The **safer commercial choice**, especially in the transition zone and around Accra. Its higher survival makes up for its lower egg output. Accepted by consumers, though some Ghanaian buyers prefer *A. achatina*. |
| ***Lissachatina fulica*** | Not recommended (see the [Species Guide](./01-species-suitability-guide.md)). |

### Managing *A. achatina* successfully

- **Nursery humidity above 85 %.** Use covered plastic or wooden nursery boxes with a damp foam or coir substrate, misted twice a day.
- **Low density for juveniles.** *A. achatina* juveniles are well known for stunting when crowded. Keep nursery densities lower than you would for *A. marginata*.
- **Calcium.** Provide finely ground oyster shell or limestone all the time. Young tiger snails grow thin, fragile shells without it.
- **Expect seasonal breeding.** Most egg-laying happens in the rainy seasons (roughly April to July and September to November in the south). Time your hatchling capacity to match.

## 3. Operating Conditions: The Ghanaian Baseline

| Parameter | Recommended practice |
|-----------|----------------------|
| Site | A sheltered, gently sloping site under existing tree cover or a plantain or cocoa canopy. Avoid valley bottoms that flood. |
| Pens | Hutch boxes for breeders and nurseries; fenced mini-paddocks (with net tops) under shade for growers |
| Soil | Loamy forest topsoil, pH 6.5–7.5. Forest soils in high-rainfall zones can be **acidic** (pH below 5.5), so lime them if necessary (see [Climatic Limitations](./07-climatic-limitations.md)). |
| Feed | Cocoyam (*kontomire*) leaves, pawpaw leaves and fruit, cassava leaves (in moderation), plantain peel, ripe banana, pineapple peel, plus a concentrate with a calcium source |
| Dry-season management | Mulch, evening watering, shade nets and windbreaks against the Harmattan, which reaches the south mainly from December to February |

## 4. Market Dynamics in Ghana

### Who buys

- **Market traders**: Kumasi Central Market, Kejetia, Makola and Agbogbloshie in Accra, Techiman market.
- **Chop bars and restaurants** serving snail in light soup, palm-nut soup, *kontomire* stew and grilled kebab-style snail.
- **Roadside sellers** on the Accra–Kumasi and Kumasi–Sunyani highways, traditionally selling wild-collected snails threaded on sticks.
- **Hotels, caterers and events**, including funerals, which are major catering events in Ghanaian culture.
- **Processors** making smoked and dried snail, and a growing **mucin and cosmetics** sector.

### How prices move through the year

As in Nigeria, **wild supply drives the price cycle**:

- **Rainy seasons:** Plenty of wild snails, so lower prices.
- **Main dry season (about December to March):** Wild snails aestivate, supply falls and prices rise sharply.
- **Christmas, Easter and the funeral season** add peaks in demand.

Ghana's long-term policy and NGO interest in snail farming has partly been a **conservation response**: over-collection of wild snails and forest loss have reduced wild populations. That gives farmed snails a good long-term market, but you are still competing with cheap wild supply in the rainy season.

## 5. The Wider Coastal Belt

### Côte d'Ivoire

- One of the largest markets for *escargot géant* in the region. Abidjan's demand is especially strong.
- Southern Côte d'Ivoire (Abidjan, San-Pédro, Gagnoa, Daloa) is humid forest and well suited to *A. achatina* and *A. marginata*.
- The north (Korhogo, Ferkessédougou) is Sudanian savanna and unsuitable outdoors.
- Francophone extension literature uses the term **"achatiniculture"** for giant-snail farming.

### Togo and Benin

- Both lie largely in the **Dahomey Gap**. The coastal strips around Lomé and Cotonou get lower rainfall than coastal Ghana's west or Nigeria's east.
- The best snail-farming conditions are in the wetter **Plateaux Region of Togo** (Kpalimé, Badou), where the forest is mountain-influenced, and in southern and south-eastern **Benin** close to the Nigerian border (Ouémé and Plateau departments).
- Cross-border trade with Nigeria is significant. Nigerian buyers often source from southern Benin.

### Liberia and Sierra Leone

- **Very high rainfall** (often 2,500–4,000 mm+) and a short dry season make them some of the most naturally suitable countries in Africa for *A. achatina*.
- The main risks are **waterlogging, fungal problems and soil acidity**. Use raised pens, good drainage and liming.
- Formal commercial farming is still small. There are opportunities for early movers, but markets are mostly local.

---

## 6. Regulation and Support

- In Ghana, snail farming falls under the **Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA)** for extension support. **Wildlife Division** rules may apply to collecting snails from the wild and to wildlife farming. Check the current position before collecting wild breeding stock.
- Exporting processed snail requires compliance with the **Ghana Food and Drugs Authority (FDA)** and the importing country's rules.
- University animal-science departments and agricultural research institutes in Ghana have published widely on *A. achatina* husbandry. They are good sources of locally tested feed formulations.
