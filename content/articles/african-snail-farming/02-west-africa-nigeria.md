# West Africa I: Snail Farming in Nigeria

> **Part 2 of the series:** *[Snail Farming Across African Regions](./00-series-hub.md)*

**Key takeaways**

- Nigeria is **Africa's largest snail market**. Traditional demand far exceeds wild supply, and the gap is widening as forests are cleared and wild snails over-collected.
- The **southern rainforest and derived-savanna states** provide near-ideal conditions. The **northern Sudan and Sahel savannas** do not support outdoor GALS farming.
- **The Harmattan** (roughly November to February) is the single biggest climatic threat, even in the south. Plan for it from the start.
- **Wild supply falls in the dry season, so prices rise.** Farms that can harvest between November and March earn the best margins.

---

## 1. Ecological Zones and Snail Suitability

Nigeria runs from humid mangrove and rainforest on the Atlantic coast to semi-desert near the Niger border. This north–south gradient decides almost everything about snail farming.

| Ecological zone | Representative states | Annual rainfall (approx.) | Dry season | Outdoor GALS suitability |
|-----------------|----------------------|---------------------------|------------|--------------------------|
| **Mangrove and freshwater swamp** | Bayelsa, Rivers, Delta (coastal), Lagos (coastal), Akwa Ibom (coastal) | 2,000–4,000 mm | 1–2 months | ★★★★☆ Excellent climate, but **flooding and waterlogged soils** need raised pens |
| **Lowland rainforest** | Edo, Ondo, Ogun, Cross River, Akwa Ibom, Imo, Abia, Anambra, Ekiti, Osun | 1,500–2,500 mm | 2–3 months | ★★★★★ **Prime snail country** |
| **Derived (forest-savanna) savanna** | Oyo, Kwara (south), Enugu, Ebonyi, Kogi (south), Benue (south) | 1,200–1,500 mm | 3–4 months | ★★★☆☆ Good with shade and dry-season irrigation |
| **Southern Guinea savanna** | Niger, Kwara (north), Kogi (north), Benue, Nasarawa, FCT | 1,000–1,300 mm | 5 months | ★★☆☆☆ Marginal. Needs housing or a strong moisture-retention system. |
| **Jos Plateau (montane)** | Plateau (Jos, Bukuru), parts of the Mambilla Plateau (Taraba) | 1,200–1,500 mm | 5 months | ★☆☆☆☆ **Too cold at night** in the dry season; Harmattan nights can fall well below 15 °C |
| **Northern Guinea savanna** | Kaduna, southern Kano, Bauchi, Gombe, Adamawa | 800–1,100 mm | 6 months | ★☆☆☆☆ Unsuitable outdoors |
| **Sudan and Sahel savanna** | Sokoto, Kebbi, Zamfara, Katsina, Kano, Jigawa, Yobe, Borno | 300–800 mm | 7–9 months | ☆☆☆☆☆ **Not viable** without full indoor climate control |

### What this means in practice

- In the **lowland rainforest states**, a well-shaded outdoor pen with basic dry-season watering keeps snails active for 9–11 months of the year.
- In the **derived savanna**, the same pen keeps snails active for 6–8 months unless the farmer irrigates and mulches heavily.
- In **FCT (Abuja) and the Middle Belt**, many successful farms use **indoor or semi-indoor systems**: converted rooms, tiered hutch boxes and trench pens inside covered structures.
- North of about 10° N, outdoor GALS farming is not realistic. See [Climatic Limitations](./07-climatic-limitations.md).

## 2. The Harmattan: Nigeria's Biggest Snail-Farming Hazard

The Harmattan is a dry, dusty north-easterly trade wind that blows from the Sahara between about **late November and February**. Its effects reach well into southern Nigeria.

**What the Harmattan does to a snail farm**

- **Relative humidity crashes.** In the north it can fall below 20 % during the day. In the south it can drop into the 30–50 % range on Harmattan afternoons.
- **Large day–night temperature swings.** Cold mornings (sometimes 12–16 °C in the Middle Belt) follow hot, dry afternoons.
- **Dust coats feed and soil**, reducing feeding.
- **Snails aestivate.** Growth stops, egg-laying stops, and young snails can die of dehydration within days.

**Harmattan-proofing checklist**

- [ ] Put **windbreaks** on the north and east sides of the farm: dense hedges, block walls or shade-net screens.
- [ ] Use **double shading**: a tree canopy (plantain, banana, *Gliricidia*) plus a thatch or shade-net roof.
- [ ] Spread **thick mulch** (dry leaves, plantain trash) over the pen soil and wet it every evening.
- [ ] **Water in the evening.** Snails are active at night, and evening watering loses less to evaporation.
- [ ] Bring hatchlings and juveniles **indoors** or into covered tiers for the Harmattan months.
- [ ] Give **water-rich feeds**: pawpaw, cucumber, watermelon rind, cocoyam leaves.

## 3. Species Choice in Nigeria

| Species | Recommendation for Nigeria |
|---------|----------------------------|
| ***Archachatina marginata*** | **First choice for almost every Nigerian farm.** It is native to the south, hardy, sells everywhere and copes with short Harmattan periods better than *A. achatina*. |
| ***Achatina achatina*** | Only for the wettest southern states (Cross River, Akwa Ibom, Rivers, Bayelsa, southern Edo and Delta), or inside climate-controlled houses. Commands premium sizes. |
| ***Lissachatina fulica*** | **Not recommended.** It is smaller, prices are lower and it carries invasive-pest and parasite risks. |

> **Tip:** The pale-fleshed *A. marginata* variety (often sold as "white meat" or *ovum*) attracts a premium from some buyers in Lagos and the South-West who prefer its appearance. Ask your buyers before choosing breeders.

## 4. Operating Conditions: The Nigerian Baseline

| Parameter | Target in Nigeria |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Pen type | Hutch boxes (tiered wooden boxes), concrete or block trench pens, "free-range" fenced paddocks with mesh tops, or converted rooms |
| Soil | Loamy topsoil from **untreated, unfertilised land**. Sieve it and check for ants. pH 6.5–7.5. |
| Soil depth | 25–30 cm in pens where snails lay eggs |
| Stocking density (indicative) | Breeders about 8–10 per m²; growers about 15–30 per m²; hatchlings about 100–300 per m² in nursery boxes |
| Feed | Greens (pawpaw leaves, cocoyam leaves, *Talinum* or "waterleaf", lettuce, cabbage waste), fruits (pawpaw, banana, pineapple peel), plus a **concentrate** of maize bran, soybean or groundnut cake, and a **calcium source** (oyster shell, bone meal, limestone) |
| Calcium | Essential for shell growth. Calcium-rich concentrate rations are commonly reported to improve growth. |
| Water | Clean shallow dishes or water-soaked foam. Let chlorinated tap water stand for 24 hours before use. |
| Predator control | Soldier ants, rats, lizards, crabs (in coastal and swamp areas), birds and **humans (theft)**. Use mesh, ant moats and a fenced site. |

## 5. Market Dynamics

### Who buys snails in Nigeria?

- **Open-market traders** in big markets in Lagos, Ibadan, Benin City, Port Harcourt, Enugu, Onitsha and Abuja, who buy live snails in bulk and resell.
- **"Peppered snail" vendors, bars and restaurants.** Peppered snail is a staple of Nigerian bar and party culture, especially in Lagos and Port Harcourt.
- **Hotels and event caterers**: weddings, chieftaincy ceremonies, owambe parties.
- **Health-conscious consumers.** Snail meat is promoted as high in protein and iron and low in fat and cholesterol.
- **Processors** who sell frozen, smoked, dried or vacuum-packed snail meat, and a growing **snail mucin** sector supplying cosmetics.
- **Diaspora channels.** Processed (cooked, smoked or dried) snail meat sent to the UK, US and Europe. Exporters must comply with the importing country's food-safety rules. **Live GALS are prohibited** in many importing countries, including the United States.

### How prices move through the year

| Period | Wild supply | Farm-gate prices |
|--------|-------------|------------------|
| **April–October (rainy season)** | High. Wild snails are collected from bush and farmland in large numbers. | **Lowest.** Farmed snails compete with cheap wild snails. |
| **November–March (dry season and Harmattan)** | Very low. Wild snails are aestivating and hard to find. | **Highest.** Many traders report prices doubling or more compared with the rainy season. |
| **December festive season** | Very low | Peak demand from Christmas and New Year events |

**Strategic implication:** Plan breeding so your **largest batch of market-size snails is ready between November and March**. Farms that sell into this window regularly report much better margins than those selling in the rainy season. Under Nigerian conditions this usually means keeping snails active through the dry season with shading and irrigation.

### How snails are sold

- **By the piece, by size grade.** Common trade terms include "small", "medium", "big" and "jumbo" (or "Agric jumbo"). Grades differ between markets, so agree definitions with your buyers.
- **By weight.** This is becoming more common with processors and formal buyers.
- **Breeding stock and hatchlings.** These are sold to new farmers at a premium. This is a legitimate income stream, but make sure your revenue does not depend mainly on recruiting new farmers.

### Price-check advice

Naira prices change quickly with inflation and currency movements. Before building a business plan:

1. Visit **at least three** local markets and price each size grade.
2. Ask the same traders what they paid during the **previous dry season**.
3. Contact two or three restaurants or peppered-snail vendors and ask about weekly volumes and preferred sizes.

## 6. Regulation and Support

- Snail farming in Nigeria is generally treated as **livestock or mini-livestock** production. Check with your **State Ministry of Agriculture** about any registration, especially if you plan to export or process.
- **Export of processed snail meat** requires compliance with Nigerian export procedures (contact the **Nigerian Export Promotion Council (NEPC)** and the **National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC)** for packaged foods) and with the importing country's food-safety rules.
- Research and extension material on snail husbandry is available from Nigerian universities with animal-science faculties and from agricultural research institutes. Ask your state's **Agricultural Development Programme (ADP)** for local training.

---

## 7. Common Mistakes on Nigerian Snail Farms

1. **Building pens in full sun** or under a metal roof with no ceiling. Pen temperatures can go above 35 °C.
2. **Using soil from fertilised or pesticide-treated farmland**, which kills snails.
3. **Ignoring soldier ants.** One raid can wipe out a nursery overnight.
4. **Overstocking** to speed up output, which leads to stunted growth, cannibalism of eggs and disease.
5. **Starting with *A. achatina*** in the derived or Guinea savanna.
6. **No dry-season plan**, so the farm earns nothing in the months when prices are highest.
7. **No security.** Snail theft is common. Fence the site and lock the nursery.
