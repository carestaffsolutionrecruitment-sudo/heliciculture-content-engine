# Mitigation Systems: Indoor Climate Control, Greenhouse Tunnels and Moisture Retention

> **Part 9 of the series:** *[Snail Farming Across African Regions](./00-series-hub.md)*

**Key takeaways**

- **Match the fix to the limitation.** Dry air needs moisture management, cold needs insulation and heat, and bad soil needs correction or replacement.
- **Start with the cheapest effective layer.** Shade and mulch come before misting, and misting comes before full climate control.
- **Greenhouses in the tropics are a heat trap.** An unshaded plastic tunnel can kill snails within hours. Shade and ventilation are not optional.
- **Every engineered system adds running costs** (water, electricity, labour). Model them against realistic **dry-season prices** before building.

---

## 1. The Mitigation Ladder

Climb only as high as your [site diagnostic score](./08-site-suitability-diagnostic-checklist.md) requires.

| Level | System | Solves | Typical capital cost | Running cost | Suitable site rating |
|-------|--------|--------|----------------------|--------------|----------------------|
| **1** | Shade, windbreaks, mulch | Short dry spells, sun, wind | Low | Very low | Excellent / Good |
| **2** | Evening irrigation and drip lines | A 3–4 month dry season | Low–medium | Low (water) | Good / Marginal |
| **3** | Covered pens with misting | Long dry season, moderate RH deficit | Medium | Medium (water, pump power) | Marginal |
| **4** | Shaded polytunnel with fogging and ventilation | Long dry season, wind, mild cold | Medium–high | Medium | Marginal / Poor |
| **5** | Insulated indoor house with humidity and temperature control | Arid zones, high altitude, cold nights | High | High (energy) | Poor |
| **6** | Fully automated climate room (sensors, controllers, backup power) | Desert, frost, intensive year-round production | Very high | Very high | Poor / Unsuitable (if economic at all) |

## 2. Moisture-Retention Systems

These are the foundation of every snail farm, including farms in good climates.

### 2.1 Shade

- **Living shade:** Plantain, banana, cocoyam, *Gliricidia*, *Leucaena* or cocoa canopy over or beside pens. Plantain and banana also provide feed and give off moisture through their leaves.
- **Built shade:** Shade net (commonly **50–70 %** shade factor), palm-frond or grass thatch, or a raised roof with a **ceiling or insulated layer** under any metal sheeting.
- **Rule:** Snail pens should **never receive direct midday sun**.

### 2.2 Windbreaks

- Plant or build windbreaks on the **side of the prevailing dry wind**. In West Africa this is **north and north-east** (the Harmattan).
- Options: dense hedges (for example *Tithonia*, which also gives some feed, or *Gliricidia*), block or mud walls, or shade-net screens 2–3 m high.
- A windbreak protects a strip roughly **5–10 times its height** downwind.

### 2.3 Mulch and substrate management

| Material | Benefit | Caution |
|----------|---------|---------|
| Dry leaves (untreated) | Cheap; holds moisture; snails shelter under it | Replace before it moulds |
| Plantain and banana trash (dried leaves, pseudo-stem slices) | Holds moisture well; snails eat it | Remove decaying material every week |
| Coconut coir | Excellent water retention in nurseries and hutch boxes | Rinse first to remove salts |
| Straw or grass | Good insulation | Check it has not been sprayed with herbicide |
| Wood shavings or sawdust | **Avoid** | Some woods are toxic; dries out snails |

- Keep **5–10 cm of mulch** over pen soil during the dry season.
- **Wet the mulch in the evening.** Snails are active at night, and evening water evaporates more slowly.

### 2.4 Irrigation

| Method | How it works | Best for |
|--------|--------------|----------|
| **Watering can or hose** (evening) | Manual wetting of soil and mulch | Small farms (under ~50 m² of pens) |
| **Drip lines** | Slow, steady soil moisture | Outdoor pens and paddocks |
| **Clay-pot (olla) irrigation** | Buried unglazed pots filled with water slowly seep into the soil | Low-cost, water-efficient option for dry areas |
| **Micro-sprinklers and misters** | Fine water droplets raise RH and wet surfaces | Covered pens and tunnels |
| **Wet foam or sponge pads** | Placed in hutch boxes as water sources and humidity buffers | Nurseries and breeder boxes |

**Water quality**

- Use clean borehole, well or rain water.
- **Chlorinated tap water:** Let it stand uncovered for 24 hours, or use a dechlorinating filter.
- **Never use salty or brackish water.** Test borehole water near the coast.
- Avoid water contaminated with pesticides or fuel.

### 2.5 Rainwater harvesting

- Collect roof runoff in tanks during the rains to irrigate during the dry season.
- **Rough sizing guide:** Each **1 mm of rain on 1 m² of roof yields about 1 litre**. A 100 m² roof in a 1,500 mm rainfall zone could collect on the order of **120,000 litres a year** after losses, which is enough to support dry-season misting for a small farm.

## 3. Greenhouse and Polytunnel Systems

Tunnels are widely promoted for snail farming in marginal zones such as the Middle Belt of Nigeria, the Accra Plains and the East African highlands. **Done well, they extend the active season dramatically. Done badly, they kill snails.**

### 3.1 The tropical heat trap

A clear, unshaded plastic tunnel in tropical sun can easily exceed **40–45 °C inside** at midday. GALS in such a tunnel will aestivate at best, and at worst die of heat stress and dehydration.

### 3.2 Design principles for snail tunnels

| Element | Specification |
|---------|---------------|
| **Covering** | UV-stabilised polythene **plus an external shade net** (50–70 %) over the top, or a shade-net-only "shade house" in hot lowlands |
| **Orientation** | Long axis **east–west** to reduce the area facing low morning and evening sun. Check local wind direction for ventilation. |
| **Height** | At least 2.5–3 m at the ridge. Taller tunnels buffer heat better. |
| **Ventilation** | **Roll-up side walls** and/or ridge vents. Open them in the daytime and close them at night (and in the Harmattan) to hold humidity. |
| **Insect and predator netting** | Fine mesh on every opening to keep out birds, lizards, rats and flies |
| **Double-door entry** | Reduces humidity loss and stops escapes |
| **Floor** | Earthen pens inside, or raised hutch tiers. Concrete paths for hygiene. |
| **Humidification** | Misting or fogging nozzles on a timer, cycled during the evening and at night, with short daytime pulses in hot weather |
| **Monitoring** | A thermo-hygrometer at snail level, with a **high-temperature alarm** if possible |

### 3.3 Operating rules for tunnels

- **Target inside conditions:** 24–30 °C and 80–90 % RH.
- If the inside temperature passes **32 °C**, **open the sides fully, increase misting and check the shade cover**.
- **Avoid constant saturation.** Air that is permanently at 100 % RH with no airflow encourages fungal growth, bacterial disease and ammonia build-up. Snails need **damp, not dripping**.
- **Clean regularly.** Remove uneaten feed every day, and replace or sanitise substrate on a regular schedule.

### 3.4 Tunnels in cool highlands

At altitude (East Africa above about 1,400 m, the Cameroon highlands, the Jos Plateau, the Zimbabwean plateau), a tunnel **traps daytime warmth** and helps keep nights warmer.

- Use **less shade** than in the lowlands (for example 30–40 %), adjusted to keep daytime temperatures below 30 °C.
- **Close the tunnel completely at dusk** to hold heat.
- Add **thermal mass** inside: water drums painted black, block walls, or stone or earth floors. These absorb heat in the day and release it at night.
- Insulate the **north side** in the Southern Hemisphere, or the **south side** in the Northern Hemisphere highlands, if it gets little sun.

## 4. Indoor Climate-Controlled Housing

This is for sites with **long cold seasons, frost, severe aridity or very low humidity**, and for intensive farms that want production all year round.

### 4.1 Building options

| Option | Notes |
|--------|-------|
| **Converted room or outbuilding** | The cheapest start. Insulate the roof and seal gaps. Avoid uninsulated metal roofs. |
| **Purpose-built block or mud-brick house** | Thick walls give **thermal mass** that buffers day–night swings. Use a ceiling with insulation (straw, reed or foam board). |
| **Insulated container or panel room** | Good control, but expensive and prone to overheating without ventilation |
| **Partially sunken (earth-sheltered) house** | The ground keeps temperatures stable. Useful in arid and cold zones, but drainage must be excellent. |

### 4.2 Interior layout

- **Tiered hutch boxes or racks** (3–5 tiers) on metal or treated-wood frames, using the floor space efficiently.
- Separate areas for **breeders, nurseries (hatchlings), growers and quarantine**.
- Washable surfaces, floor drains and good lighting for inspections. Snails do not need much light; a natural day length of about 12 hours, or indirect light, is enough.

### 4.3 Humidity control

| Equipment | Notes |
|-----------|-------|
| **Ultrasonic humidifiers or foggers** | Fine mist; low energy per unit. Use clean or filtered water to avoid mineral dust. |
| **High-pressure misting systems** | Effective for large rooms. Needs a pump, filters and a timer or humidistat. |
| **Evaporative cooler pads (wet walls)** | In hot, dry climates (the Sahel, northern Nigeria, northern Kenya), these **cool and humidify at the same time**. Very effective where the outside air is dry. |
| **Humidistat controller** | Switches humidifiers to hold RH at **80–90 %** |

### 4.4 Temperature control

| Need | Equipment |
|------|-----------|
| **Heating** (highlands, Highveld, cold nights) | Thermostatically controlled heaters, radiant tube heaters, or heat mats under hutches. Heat-retaining water tanks or masonry stoves are options. **Avoid un-vented combustion heaters**: carbon monoxide and dry air both harm snails. |
| **Cooling** (arid lowlands, hot seasons) | Evaporative cooling, insulation, ventilation fans, roof reflectors, shade over the building |
| **Controller** | A thermostat holding **24–28 °C**, with high and low alarms |

### 4.5 Ventilation

Enclosed rooms build up **ammonia, carbon dioxide and odours** from snail waste and rotting feed. Provide:

- **Low inlets and high exhaust vents**, with insect mesh.
- **Small exhaust fans** on timers, or linked to humidity and temperature.
- A balance: enough air exchange to keep the air fresh **without** drying out the room.

### 4.6 Power and resilience

- In many African locations, **grid power is unreliable**. A humidifier failure during a Harmattan afternoon can cause losses within hours.
- Consider **solar PV with battery storage** for fans, pumps and controllers. Heaters draw a lot of power, so lower the heating load with insulation and thermal mass first.
- Keep a **manual backup**: watering cans, wet sacks, and a drill for staff on what to do when the power fails.

### 4.7 Economics: a hard look

Before building a climate-controlled house, calculate:

1. **Capital cost** of the building, equipment and installation.
2. **Monthly running cost** of power, water, filters and maintenance.
3. **Expected output**: kilograms of live snails per year at realistic survival and growth rates.
4. **Selling price**, using **conservative** estimates and checking against actual buyers.

> **Rule of thumb:** If your local market cannot pay **more** than the price of snails trucked in from a humid region **plus your extra running costs**, indoor production in a poor climate will not pay. In that case, consider becoming a **trader, aggregator or processor** sourcing from farms in suitable zones.

## 5. Soil Correction

### 5.1 Correcting acidic soils (pH below 6.0)

| Amendment | Notes |
|-----------|-------|
| **Agricultural lime (calcium carbonate)** | The standard correction. Mix it in thoroughly and **let the soil settle for 2–4 weeks** before stocking. Follow soil-test recommendations for the rate. |
| **Wood ash** (from untreated wood) | Raises pH and supplies potassium and calcium. Use sparingly, because it is strongly alkaline when fresh. |
| **Crushed oyster or snail shell, eggshell** | Slowly raises pH and supplies calcium. Also useful as a feed supplement. |
| **Dolomitic lime** | Supplies magnesium as well as calcium. Useful on magnesium-poor soils. |

- **Never use quicklime or hydrated (slaked) lime directly in occupied pens.** They are caustic. Use them only for disinfecting empty pens, then rinse and rest the pen before restocking.
- Re-test the pH **after 4 weeks** and again every few months, because high rainfall gradually re-acidifies soil.

### 5.2 Correcting alkaline or saline soils

- Mild alkalinity (7.6–8.0) is usually tolerable.
- For high pH or salinity, **do not try to correct it in place**. **Replace the pen soil** with good loam brought in from elsewhere, and use hutch boxes.

### 5.3 Correcting texture

- **Sandy soil:** Blend in loam and well-rotted compost (roughly 1 part compost to 3 parts soil).
- **Clay soil:** Blend in sand and compost, and raise pens to improve drainage.
- **Laterite or hardpan:** Build raised trench pens or hutch boxes and fill them with imported topsoil.

### 5.4 Soil hygiene

- **Sieve** soil to remove stones, sticks, ants and insect larvae.
- **Sterilise** new soil by sun-drying it on plastic sheeting for several days, or by gentle heat treatment, before using it in nurseries.
- **Replace** pen soil every **3–6 months** (more often in very wet climates) to prevent disease and parasite build-up.

## 6. Region-by-Region Mitigation Quick Reference

| Location type | Primary limitation | Recommended mitigation |
|---------------|--------------------|------------------------|
| Southern Nigeria, southern Ghana, coastal Cameroon | Harmattan dryness; heavy-rain waterlogging | Level 1–2: shade, windbreaks, mulch, evening watering, raised pens |
| Nigeria's Middle Belt and FCT; central Ghana; the Accra Plains | Long dry season; low RH | Level 3–4: covered pens or shaded tunnels with misting |
| Northern Nigeria, northern Ghana, the Sahel | Aridity; extreme low RH | Level 5–6: enclosed evaporative-cooled and humidified house, **or do not farm** |
| Jos Plateau; the Cameroon Western Highlands | Cool dry-season nights | Level 4–5: closed tunnels with thermal mass, or insulated houses |
| Nairobi and the central Kenyan highlands; western Uganda highlands; Rwanda | Cold nights (June to August) and dry spells | Level 5: insulated, heated, humidified housing |
| Kenyan coast; the Lake Victoria basin | Dry seasons | Level 2–3 |
| South Africa's Highveld | Frost; dry winters | Level 6: fully enclosed, heated house |
| KwaZulu-Natal coast; the Mpumalanga lowveld | Cool dry winter | Level 3–4 |
