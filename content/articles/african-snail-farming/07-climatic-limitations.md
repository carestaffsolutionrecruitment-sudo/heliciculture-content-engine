# Climatic Limitations: Where Giant African Land Snails Do Not Thrive

> **Part 7 of the series:** *[Snail Farming Across African Regions](./00-series-hub.md)*

**Key takeaways**

- Giant African land snails (GALS) are **moisture-limited animals first and temperature-limited animals second**. Most failed farms are failures of humidity.
- Five hard limits define where GALS will not thrive: **aridity, low relative humidity, cold (usually due to altitude), unsuitable soil (pH, texture, contamination) and exposure (wind, sun, heat)**.
- Snails do not die quickly in poor conditions. **They shut down.** A farm can look alive but earn nothing for half the year because its snails are dormant.
- Every limit can be **reduced by engineering**, but each fix has a running cost. In some places the honest answer is **"do not farm GALS here"**.

---

## 1. The Physiology Behind the Limits

Knowing *why* snails fail makes it much easier to diagnose a site.

### Water: the snail's most important resource

- A snail's soft body is **mostly water** and is covered by permeable skin. It loses moisture all the time by evaporation.
- **Moving costs water.** Snails glide on a layer of mucus that is itself mostly water, so every metre travelled to find food costs moisture.
- When the air is dry, a snail stops moving and feeding, withdraws into its shell and seals the opening with a dried mucus membrane, the **epiphragm**. This is **aestivation**.
- A dormant snail lives on its body reserves. It **loses weight, does not grow and does not lay eggs**. Long or repeated aestivation weakens it and can kill hatchlings and juveniles.

### Temperature: a cold-blooded animal

- Snails are **ectotherms**. Their metabolism, feeding and reproduction depend on the surrounding temperature.
- **Below about 15 °C**, GALS feed slowly. **Below about 10–12 °C** they generally become dormant (hibernation). Long exposure near freezing or to **any frost** is likely to kill tropical species.
- **Above about 32–35 °C**, heat stress and fast water loss trigger aestivation. In direct sun, shell temperatures can quickly reach lethal levels.

### Calcium and soil chemistry

- The shell is mostly **calcium carbonate**. Snails get calcium from feed and from the soil they eat and rest in.
- **Acidic soils** are usually low in calcium, and acidic conditions make it harder to keep the shell strong. You see **thin, brittle or pitted shells** and slow growth.
- Snails lay their eggs **in the soil**. Soil that is too dry, too wet, compacted or contaminated reduces hatching.

## 2. Limitation 1: Arid and Desert Zones

### Where in Africa

| Arid region | Countries / areas |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **The Sahara** | Mauritania, Western Sahara, northern Mali, Niger, Chad, Algeria, Libya, Egypt, northern Sudan |
| **The Sahel** | Senegal (north), southern Mauritania, central Mali, Burkina Faso (north), Niger (south), northern Nigeria (Sokoto to Borno), northern Cameroon (Far North), Chad (central), Sudan (central) |
| **The Horn of Africa** | Somalia, Djibouti, eastern Ethiopia (Afar, Somali Region), north and north-east Kenya |
| **The south-west** | Namib Desert, Kalahari (Botswana, eastern Namibia, Northern Cape), the Karoo |

### Why snails fail

- Annual rainfall below about **800–1,000 mm**, concentrated in a **short rainy season of 2–4 months**.
- **Very low humidity** (often 10–30 % in the daytime dry season).
- Hot days and large daily temperature swings.
- **Dust storms and dry winds** (the Harmattan in West Africa, and the *khamsin* and other desert winds further north).

### Diagnostic signs

- Natural vegetation is grassland, thorn scrub or desert. There are **no wild GALS** in the surrounding bush.
- The dry season lasts **5 months or more**.
- Exposed soil cracks or turns to dust in the dry season.

### Verdict

**Outdoor GALS farming is not viable.** The only option is a **fully enclosed, climate-controlled house** (see [Mitigation Systems](./09-mitigation-systems.md)). Economic viability then depends on cheap water and energy and on a market strong enough to pay for them. In most Sahelian locations, **buying snails from the humid south is cheaper than producing them locally**.

## 3. Limitation 2: Insufficient Sustained Relative Humidity

This is the **most common hidden limitation**. It affects many sites that *look* green, such as savanna farmland, urban plots and coastal savanna.

### Thresholds

| Mean daily RH (in the pen, at night) | Effect on GALS |
|--------------------------------------|----------------|
| **80–95 %** | Optimal. Snails feed, grow and breed. |
| **70–79 %** | Feeding falls; *A. achatina* hatchlings struggle |
| **60–69 %** | Frequent aestivation; poor breeding; deaths among the young |
| **Below 60 % for more than a few days** | Most snails aestivate. Hatchlings and juveniles die. |

**The key word is "sustained".** A site that reaches 90 % RH at dawn but drops to 35 % every afternoon for four months is **not** suitable without intervention. The dry hours are long enough to trigger aestivation and stop growth.

### Where in Africa this is the main constraint

- West Africa's **derived and Guinea savanna** (central Nigeria, central Ghana, central Côte d'Ivoire, Togo and Benin inland).
- The **Dahomey Gap** coastal savanna (the Accra Plains, coastal Togo and Benin).
- The **Middle Belt** and **FCT** in Nigeria.
- **Zambia, Zimbabwe and Malawi** in their long dry season (about May to October).
- **East African dry seasons** (about January to February and June to September).

### Diagnostic check

1. Buy a **digital thermo-hygrometer**, preferably a data-logging one, and place it **inside the pen at snail level**, in shade.
2. Record **minimum, maximum and average RH** over at least **2–4 weeks in the driest month**. Local weather station data help too, but pen-level readings matter most.
3. If the **night-time average falls below 75 %**, or the **daytime minimum falls below 50 %** on more than a third of days, the site needs **moisture-retention systems** at minimum.

## 4. Limitation 3: High-Altitude and Cold Regions

### The altitude rule of thumb

Temperature falls by roughly **6–6.5 °C per 1,000 m** of elevation. Near the equator:

| Elevation | Typical outcome for GALS (tropical Africa) |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------|
| 0–1,000 m | Generally warm enough; humidity is the limiting factor |
| 1,000–1,400 m | Usually acceptable. Some slowing on cool nights. |
| 1,400–1,800 m | **Stress zone.** Frequent cold-night dormancy and slower growth. Housing is recommended. |
| Above 1,800 m | **Unsuitable outdoors.** Fully housed and heated systems only. |

Further from the equator (Southern Africa, North Africa), **winter cold and frost** set in at much lower altitudes.

### Cold regions in Africa where GALS do not thrive outdoors

| Region | Notes |
|--------|-------|
| **Ethiopian Highlands** (Addis Ababa at about 2,350 m and most of central Ethiopia) | Cool all year; cold nights |
| **East African highlands** (Nairobi upper, central Kenya, Kericho, Eldoret, western Uganda highlands) | Cool nights, especially June to August |
| **Rwanda and Burundi** | Almost entirely above 1,400 m |
| **Eastern DRC highlands** (Kivu) | High altitude |
| **Western Highlands of Cameroon** (higher parts) and the **Jos and Mambilla Plateaus** (Nigeria) | Cool dry-season nights |
| **Southern African Highveld** (Gauteng, the Free State) | Frosty winters |
| **Lesotho and the Drakensberg** | Snow and frost |
| **The Maghreb** (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia) | Cool winters. Unsuitable for GALS, but suitable for *Helix* and *Cornu* species, which are farmed there. |
| **Western Cape** (winter-rainfall) | Too cool for GALS; suitable for *Cornu aspersum* |

### Diagnostic signs

- **Night temperatures** below 15 °C for more than about 4–6 weeks a year.
- **Any frost** at all, even occasional ground frost in valley bottoms.
- Snails become **sluggish in the morning** and only start feeding in the warm part of the day.
- Eggs take much longer to hatch, or fail to hatch.

### Verdict

- **Occasional cool nights:** Use insulated housing and thermal mass.
- **Long cold seasons or frost:** Use fully enclosed, insulated and heated houses, or switch to a **cool-climate species** where permitted.

## 5. Limitation 4: Unsuitable Soil

### Soil pH

| Soil pH | Effect | Typical African locations |
|---------|--------|---------------------------|
| **Below 5.5** (strongly acidic) | Low calcium, brittle shells, slow growth, poor egg hatch | High-rainfall forest soils (Liberia, Sierra Leone, coastal Cameroon), leached laterites, tea-growing highlands (Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda), peaty swamp soils |
| **5.5–6.4** (moderately acidic) | Acceptable with calcium supplements | Much of the humid forest belt |
| **6.5–7.5** (neutral) | **Optimal** | Good forest loam, many river-valley soils |
| **7.6–8.5** (mildly alkaline) | Usually acceptable | Limestone areas, some savanna soils |
| **Above 8.5** (strongly alkaline, often saline or sodic) | Poor. Often linked to **salt**, which snails do not tolerate. | Arid zones, poorly drained irrigated land, coastal salt flats |

### Soil texture and structure

| Problem | Effect | Fix |
|---------|--------|-----|
| **Pure sand** (coastal sands, Kalahari sand) | Dries out fast; eggs desiccate; burrows collapse | Mix in loam and compost; mulch; consider hutch boxes with imported soil |
| **Heavy clay** | Waterlogs, then sets hard and cracks; snails cannot dig to lay eggs | Mix with sand and organic matter; raise pens |
| **Laterite and hardpan** (ironstone gravel common in West African savannas) | Shallow, hard and infertile | Use imported topsoil in boxes or trenches; do not farm on bare laterite |
| **Waterlogged or swampy ground** | Snails drown; fungal and bacterial problems | Raise pens on stilts or mounds; dig drainage channels |

### Contaminated soils: a food-safety limit

Snails **accumulate heavy metals and other pollutants** from soil and feed, and those pollutants end up in the meat people eat. **Do not farm on or use soil from:**

- **Oil-polluted land.** This is relevant to parts of the **Niger Delta**, where hydrocarbon contamination of soils and water is well documented.
- **Mining areas and mine tailings**, for example artisanal gold-mining areas with mercury and other metals.
- **Roadsides and industrial sites** (lead, cadmium, hydrocarbons).
- **Fields recently sprayed with pesticides or herbicides**, or treated with chemical fertilisers.
- **Old dump sites.**

### Diagnostic check

1. **pH test:** Use a soil test kit or pH meter, or send a sample to an agricultural laboratory. Test samples from several points and depths (0–15 cm and 15–30 cm).
2. **Squeeze test:** Squeeze a handful of moist soil. Good snail soil **holds its shape and then crumbles** when you press it. Pure sand falls apart. Clay forms a sticky ribbon.
3. **Drainage test:** Dig a hole 30 cm deep, fill it with water and time how long it takes to drain. **Longer than about 4 hours** suggests poor drainage. **Under 10 minutes** suggests sand that is too free-draining.
4. **History check:** Ask what was grown on the land, what was sprayed, and whether there was ever an oil spill, mining or dumping nearby.

## 6. Limitation 5: Exposure and Problem Micro-Climates

Even inside a suitable region, **the micro-climate of a particular plot** can make or break a farm.

| Micro-climate hazard | Why it fails | How to spot it |
|----------------------|--------------|----------------|
| **Open, sun-exposed plots** | Pen surfaces get far hotter than the air; fast moisture loss | No tree canopy; soil hot to the touch at midday |
| **Corrugated iron roofs without a ceiling** | Radiant heat; interior temperatures above 35–40 °C | Room feels like an oven by early afternoon |
| **Rooftop or upper-floor urban pens** | Urban heat island; wind exposure; no ground moisture | Hot, dry and windy |
| **Windy ridges and hilltops** | Wind strips moisture from snails and soil | Trees bent, dust, constant breeze |
| **Valley-bottom frost pockets** (in the highlands and in Southern Africa) | Cold air drains downhill and collects; frost forms | Mist or frost lingering in low spots on winter mornings |
| **Flood-prone low ground** | Snails drown; soil waterlogs | Water marks, sedges, history of flooding |
| **Coastal salt-spray zones** | Salt damages snail skin and soil | Salt crust on surfaces, stunted vegetation |
| **Sites near busy roads or factories** | Dust, pollution, vibration and noise | Dust film on leaves, exhaust smell |
| **Termite and soldier-ant hotspots** | Predation on eggs and hatchlings | Active mounds, ant trails |

## 7. Summary Matrix: Will Giant African Land Snails Thrive Here?

| Condition | ✅ Thrive | ⚠️ Possible with mitigation | ❌ Do not farm GALS outdoors |
|-----------|----------|-----------------------------|------------------------------|
| Annual rainfall | More than 1,500 mm, well spread | 1,000–1,500 mm | Under ~1,000 mm with a long dry season |
| Dry season (RH under 70 %) | 0–2 months | 3–4 months | 5+ months |
| Night-time mean RH | Above 80 % | 70–80 % | Below 70 % for long periods |
| Coolest-month mean minimum | Above 18 °C | 13–18 °C | Below ~12 °C, or any frost |
| Hottest-month mean maximum | Below 32 °C | 32–35 °C | Above 35 °C regularly |
| Elevation (tropics) | Below 1,200 m | 1,200–1,800 m | Above 1,800 m |
| Soil pH | 6.5–7.5 | 5.5–6.4 or 7.6–8.5 | Below 5.5 (uncorrected) or above 8.5 |
| Soil contamination | None | — | **Any oil, mining, pesticide or heavy-metal contamination** |

For a full scored assessment, use the [Site Suitability Diagnostic Checklist](./08-site-suitability-diagnostic-checklist.md).

## 8. Mitigation: An Overview

| Limitation | Primary mitigation | Typical cost | See |
|------------|--------------------|--------------|-----|
| Short dry season | Shade, mulch, evening irrigation, windbreaks | Low | [Mitigation Systems §2](./09-mitigation-systems.md#2-moisture-retention-systems) |
| Long dry season or low RH | Covered houses with misting, fogging and humidifiers | Medium | [Mitigation Systems §3](./09-mitigation-systems.md#3-greenhouse-and-polytunnel-systems) |
| Cold nights or altitude | Insulated housing, thermal mass, heating | Medium–high | [Mitigation Systems §4](./09-mitigation-systems.md#4-indoor-climate-controlled-housing) |
| Arid or desert | Fully enclosed climate control | High | [Mitigation Systems §4](./09-mitigation-systems.md#4-indoor-climate-controlled-housing) |
| Acidic soil | Agricultural lime, wood ash, crushed shell | Low | [Mitigation Systems §5](./09-mitigation-systems.md#5-soil-correction) |
| Poor texture | Imported loam, raised boxes, compost | Low–medium | [Mitigation Systems §5](./09-mitigation-systems.md#5-soil-correction) |
| Contaminated soil | **Relocate.** Do not try to remediate for food production. | — | — |
