# East Africa: Snail Farming in Kenya and Uganda

> **Part 5 of the series:** *[Snail Farming Across African Regions](./00-series-hub.md)*

**Key takeaways**

- East Africa lies on the equator, but **altitude, not latitude, decides snail suitability**. Nairobi (about 1,700 m) is far cooler at night than Lagos or Douala.
- The best natural zones are the **Kenyan coast**, the **Lake Victoria basin** (western Kenya and southern Uganda) and lowland pockets with **two rainy seasons a year**.
- **Roughly four-fifths of Kenya's land area is arid or semi-arid.** Outdoor GALS farming is not viable there.
- Snails are **wildlife** in Kenya, and farming them requires authorisation from the **Kenya Wildlife Service (KWS)**. Uganda has comparable wildlife-user-rights requirements under the **Uganda Wildlife Authority (UWA)**.
- **The market is still being built.** Snail meat is not a traditional food for most East African communities, so the main buyers are restaurants, hotels, the expatriate and diaspora communities, exporters and snail-mucin processors.

---

## 1. Why East Africa Is Different

In West and Central Africa, snail farmers mostly worry about the **dry season**. In East Africa they also worry about **cold nights**.

**The altitude rule of thumb:** air temperature falls by about **6–6.5 °C for every 1,000 m of altitude**. A site at 1,700 m is therefore roughly 10–11 °C cooler on average than a sea-level site at the same latitude. On clear dry-season nights the gap can be even larger.

| Location | Approx. elevation | Coolest-month mean minimum (approx.) | Snail implication |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------------------------|-------------------|
| Mombasa | ~50 m | ~22 °C | Excellent temperature all year |
| Kisumu | ~1,130 m | ~16–17 °C | Acceptable; slight slowing on cool nights |
| Kampala | ~1,200 m | ~16–17 °C | Acceptable to good |
| Nairobi | ~1,700 m | **~10–12 °C** (June to August) | **Stress zone.** Snails hibernate on cold nights without housing. |
| Kabale (SW Uganda) | ~1,900 m | ~10 °C | Unsuitable outdoors |
| Eldoret / Kericho | ~2,000–2,100 m | ~8–10 °C | Unsuitable outdoors |

*Figures are approximate long-term averages for illustration. Check local meteorological records for your exact site.*

## 2. Kenya: Zone-by-Zone Suitability

| Zone | Representative counties | Rainfall pattern | Outdoor GALS suitability |
|------|------------------------|------------------|--------------------------|
| **Coastal strip** | Mombasa, Kwale, Kilifi, Lamu (coastal belt) | Two rainy seasons; long rains about April to June | ★★★★☆ Warm and humid. Irrigate in the dry months. |
| **Lake Victoria basin (lowland)** | Kisumu, Homa Bay, Siaya, Busia, Migori | Two rainy seasons | ★★★★☆ Good, especially near the lake |
| **Western highlands, lower slopes** | Kakamega, Vihiga, Bungoma (lower areas) | High rainfall, rains for most of the year | ★★★☆☆ Wet enough, but cooler. Kakamega Forest's microclimate is favourable. |
| **Central highlands and Nairobi** | Nairobi, Kiambu, Murang'a, Nyeri, Machakos (upper) | Two rainy seasons; cool June to August | ★★☆☆☆ **Housed systems only** for reliable production |
| **High-altitude highlands** | Nyandarua, Nakuru (upper), Uasin Gishu, Kericho, Nyeri (upper) | Wet but cold | ★☆☆☆☆ Unsuitable without heated housing |
| **Rift Valley floor** | Baringo, Nakuru (lake basin), Kajiado (lower) | Semi-arid to arid | ★☆☆☆☆ Too dry |
| **Arid and semi-arid lands (ASALs)** | Turkana, Marsabit, Mandera, Wajir, Garissa, Isiolo, Samburu, Tana River (inland) | Very low, erratic rainfall | ☆☆☆☆☆ **Not viable** |

### Kenya's two rainy seasons

Kenya's **long rains** (roughly March to May) and **short rains** (roughly October to December) mean that, at a suitable altitude, snails can be active for two periods a year without irrigation. With shading and modest irrigation, the dry periods between them (January to February and June to September) can be bridged. In the highlands, June to August is both **dry and cold**, which is the hardest season for snails there.

## 3. Uganda: Zone-by-Zone Suitability

| Zone | Representative districts | Outdoor GALS suitability |
|------|-------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Lake Victoria crescent** | Kampala, Wakiso, Mukono, Buikwe, Jinja, Masaka, Kalangala (islands) | ★★★★☆ **Uganda's best snail zone.** Two rainy seasons, high humidity and mild temperatures. |
| **Central and mid-western lowlands** | Mubende, Mityana, Hoima, Kibaale, Masindi | ★★★☆☆ Good with dry-season shading and irrigation |
| **Northern Uganda** | Gulu, Lira, Kitgum, Arua | ★★☆☆☆ More pronounced single dry season (about December to March) |
| **Western highlands** | Kabale, Kisoro, Kabarole (upper), Bushenyi (upper) | ★☆☆☆☆ Too cold at altitude |
| **Mt Elgon slopes** | Mbale, Sironko, Kapchorwa | ★☆☆☆☆ to ★★☆☆☆ on the lower slopes only |
| **Karamoja (north-east)** | Moroto, Kotido, Kaabong, Napak | ☆☆☆☆☆ Semi-arid. **Not viable.** |

Uganda's position on the Lake Victoria plateau at about 1,100–1,300 m gives the **central region temperatures well suited to snails**: warm days and mild nights, with fewer temperature extremes than lowland West Africa. The limiting factor is the **dry seasons** (roughly December to February and June to August).

## 4. Species Choice in East Africa

| Species | Status and recommendation |
|---------|---------------------------|
| ***Lissachatina fulica*** | **Native to coastal East Africa.** The most readily available species locally. Fast-breeding and hardy, but smaller. Treat it as a crop pest: **contain it securely** and never release it. |
| **Other native East African land snails** (including other large achatinids and the smaller *Limicolaria* species) | Present in several areas. They are not well established as farmed species, so treat them as pilot or research options. |
| ***Archachatina marginata*** | **Not native to East Africa.** Some farms have reportedly imported it for its size and its acceptance by West African buyers. Importing a non-native species needs **explicit authorisation** from the wildlife and plant/animal health authorities. **Never import without written permits.** |
| ***Achatina achatina*** | Not native. It needs very high humidity, so it is poorly suited to most East African sites even where permitted. |

## 5. Regulation: Get Your Permits First

### Kenya

- Land snails are treated as **wildlife** under Kenyan law. Commercial snail farming has required a **wildlife farming or user-rights licence from the Kenya Wildlife Service (KWS)** under the **Wildlife Conservation and Management Act, 2013**.
- Typical requirements have included a site inspection, a farm plan, proof of the source of breeding stock, and secure (escape-proof) housing.
- **Exporting** live snails, snail meat or mucin needs further permits, including KWS export documentation and the importing country's sanitary requirements.
- **Action:** Contact your nearest KWS office **before** you buy stock or build pens. Confirm the current licence categories and fees.

### Uganda

- Commercial use of wildlife in Uganda is governed by the **Uganda Wildlife Act** and administered by the **Uganda Wildlife Authority (UWA)** through **wildlife use rights** (for example, farming and ranching).
- **Action:** Ask UWA whether your planned species and system need a wildlife-use-rights permit. Ask the **Ministry of Agriculture, Animal Industry and Fisheries (MAAIF)** about any animal-health requirements.

### Tanzania and Rwanda

- **Tanzania:** *L. fulica* is native along the coast. Consult the **Tanzania Wildlife Management Authority (TAWA)** about permits.
- **Rwanda:** The whole country is at high altitude (mostly above 1,400 m). Outdoor GALS farming is marginal everywhere, so only housed systems are realistic. Consult the **Rwanda Development Board (RDB)** about wildlife permits.

> **Warning about investment schemes:** In the 2010s and since, East Africa has seen heavily marketed snail-farming "opportunities", including starter kits, overpriced breeding stock and promises of guaranteed export buyers. **Verify any buyer independently**, ask to visit existing farms that are actually selling, and never pay for "guaranteed buy-back" contracts without legal advice.

## 6. Market Dynamics: Building Demand That Is Still Small

East Africa's snail market behaves very differently from West Africa's.

| Market channel | Notes |
|----------------|-------|
| **West African diaspora and expatriate communities** | Nigerians, Ghanaians, Cameroonians and Congolese in Nairobi and Kampala. Strong demand for large snails. |
| **Asian and European expatriate communities** | Interest in prepared escargot and in Asian-style dishes |
| **Hotels, restaurants and the tourist trade** | Coastal resorts (Mombasa, Diani, Malindi), Nairobi and Kampala fine dining. The volumes are small but premium. |
| **Snail mucin (slime) for cosmetics** | One of the **fastest-growing outlets**. Mucin extraction does not require a meat-eating local market, but it does require hygienic processing, standards compliance and real buyers. |
| **Export** | European and Asian markets for frozen or processed meat and mucin. Exporting requires sanitary certification and consistent volumes. It is **difficult for small farms** without aggregation. |
| **Animal-feed and calcium by-products** | Crushed shells as a calcium supplement for poultry feed. Snail meal has been researched as a protein source in feed. These are low value but reduce waste. |

**Key market strategy for East African start-ups**

1. **Sign up buyers before you scale.** Find a restaurant, processor or exporter willing to state in writing their volumes, grades and price.
2. **Start small**, with tens to a few hundred breeders, to learn husbandry in your microclimate.
3. **Consider value-added products** such as mucin, frozen cleaned meat or prepared escargot to make the most of a limited fresh market.
4. **Educate consumers.** Tasting events, nutritional messaging and partnerships with chefs have helped grow local acceptance.

## 7. Operating Conditions: The East African Baseline

| Parameter | Recommended practice |
|-----------|----------------------|
| Housing | In the highlands, **use enclosed, insulated rooms or greenhouses with shade cloth**. In lowland and lake zones, shaded outdoor pens with covered nurseries are enough. |
| Night temperature | Keep it above about **18 °C** for good growth and breeding. Use thermal mass, insulation or low-wattage heating in highland houses (see [Mitigation Systems](./09-mitigation-systems.md)). |
| Humidity | Mist in the evenings during the dry seasons. Use mulch and damp substrate. |
| Soil | Many highland soils are **acidic**, particularly volcanic and high-rainfall soils (for example in the tea zones). Test the pH and lime as needed. |
| Biosecurity | **Escape-proof** containment, for legal compliance and to avoid crop damage. Use double fencing, mesh tops and salt or copper barriers. |
